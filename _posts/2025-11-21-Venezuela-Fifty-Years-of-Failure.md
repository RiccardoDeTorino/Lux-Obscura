### **Introduction**

For half a century, United States policy toward Venezuela has operated as a continuous campaign of coercion disguised as diplomacy. What began as management of oil assets evolved into full-spectrum economic warfare, covert action, and military threat. This examination traces that trajectory from resource control through the Chavez rupture to the present humanitarian catastrophe. The evidence demonstrates a strategic doctrine rooted in two imperatives: absolute energy primacy and unquenchable hemispheric supremacy. This essay reconstructs how a relationship between sovereign states degenerated into a one-sided assault that killed tens of thousands, displaced millions, and failed completely at its stated objective of regime change.

### **Part One: The Architecture of Control (1919-2016)**

American involvement in Venezuelan affairs started with oil. The Wilson administration, spooked by projections showing domestic petroleum running dry, told its diplomats in 1919 to help U.S. companies grab Venezuelan concessions. Washington treated the country as a strategic reserve for its own energy security, and the 1943 Venezuelan oil law, hammered out under American mediation, locked in the “fifty-fifty” profit split. That arrangement gave Venezuela the appearance of sovereignty while U.S. multinationals ran the actual operations, and State Department memoranda from 1946 show officials tracking Venezuela’s petroleum developments with “keen and continued interest” for their “military and economic importance.” Caracas supplied energy. Washington supplied protection, mostly for the extraction apparatus itself. Nobody in either capital pretended the exchange ran both directions.

OPEC showed up in September 1960, co-founded by Venezuelan minister Juan Pablo Pérez Alfonzo, and Washington read it as an attack on the whole arrangement. A classified State Department assessment laid out three options: revise antitrust laws so the U.S. could coordinate with producers, oppose the cartel and risk handing the Soviets an opening, or engage case by case and stay neutral. The U.S. picked passive opposition, keeping up the fiction of market neutrality while officials privately seethed at oil-producing nations colluding on price. Venezuela imposed mandatory export quotas and raised taxes in December 1971, and the U.S. Embassy warned of a “showdown” threatening the “foundational operating model of international oil companies.” Creole Petroleum, an Esso subsidiary, called the measures “unacceptable” and promised to fight them. Full nationalization arrived in 1976 anyway, and Washington accepted the transfer of ownership on one condition: operational continuity had to hold. The newly formed PDVSA adopted a corporate structure built to keep politics out of management, which preserved American technical influence long after legal title changed hands. Control mattered more than ownership to U.S. policymakers. The flow of oil and the profitability of American service contractors constituted the baseline they wouldn’t budge on, and sovereign assertion was fine as long as it stayed inside those lines.

The Carter administration complicated things by pushing human rights, which created friction with Latin American authoritarian regimes, including the dictatorial government then running Venezuela. Washington could stomach anti-communist brutality. What it couldn’t stomach was instability. Vice President Nixon’s motorcade got attacked in Caracas in May 1958, and the political earthquake that followed forced a fast recalculation. The U.S. dropped the dictator Marcos Pérez Jiménez and embraced the democratic socialist Rómulo Betancourt, a pivot driven by damage control rather than any conversion of principle. American investment totaled $2.5 billion by 1957, and that money needed a stable environment to sit in. If authoritarianism kept generating popular uprisings that threatened capital, then democracy became the preferred arrangement, provided it stayed amenable to American interests. Betancourt understood the deal. He kept the oil flowing and the communists suppressed, and Washington stayed satisfied. U.S. hegemony in the region has required either a compliant authoritarian or a managed democrat, and the option of a government setting its own course has stayed off the table.

The 1989 Caracazo protests left hundreds dead after IMF-imposed austerity measures sparked a mass revolt, and Washington offered no condemnation. Economic restructuring took precedence over the body count, and the Venezuelan military handled the crisis internally, preserving the institutional framework for continued extraction. Hugo Chavez led a failed coup attempt in 1992 against President Carlos Andrés Pérez, and the U.S. watched without intervening, gauging the military’s loyalty and the regime’s capacity to save itself. Chavez’s electoral victory in 1998 broke from previous nationalist movements in a way that mattered. Previous leaders had wanted to renegotiate terms within the existing order. Chavez wanted to tear the order down.

The Clinton administration refused Chavez a visa in 1998, a preemptive judgment of unacceptability delivered before the man had taken a single official action. His early moves confirmed what Washington feared. He convened a constitutional assembly with participatory clauses that threatened elite control, and he severed four decades of military ties with the United States, cutting the connection to the School of the Americas and joint naval exercises. After September 11, 2001, he refused to back the U.S.-led war on terror, declaring publicly that “you don’t fight terror with terror,” which positioned him as an ideological adversary rather than a nationalist leader Washington could work around. His foreign policy accelerated the rupture: subsidized oil-for-services deals with Cuba, expanded trade with Iran, vocal opposition to U.S. interventions in Afghanistan and Iraq and Syria. Venezuela went from an asset to the primary target for rollback in the Americas, and the response came fast, hitting from multiple directions at once.

The National Endowment for Democracy, the Agency for International Development, and the International Republican Institute poured millions into opposition groups, media outlets, political parties, and student movements across Venezuela. They built a permanent infrastructure of resistance. U.S. politicians flew to Caracas to coach opposition leaders on electoral strategy and youth outreach. American diplomats pressed opposition figures to unify their slates and avoid splitting the anti-Chavez vote. Coordinating a foreign opposition’s electoral playbook from inside their capital is political intervention in a sovereign state’s internal affairs.

The 2002 coup attempt put that infrastructure to its first real test. Declassified documents show the CIA knew about the plot weeks before it went down. Pentagon officials admitted sending what they called “informal, subtle signals that we didn’t like this guy.” The U.S. Embassy warned Chavez. He ignored the warning, the coup proceeded, and Washington became one of only two governments on earth to recognize the illegitimate interim president Pedro Carmona. The whole thing collapsed within 48 hours. But the trust between the two governments was gone after that, and the U.S. had shown it would back extralegal regime change. Every interaction between Caracas and Washington after April 2002 carried the memory of that recognition.

The December 2002 to February 2003 oil lockout at PDVSA tried economic strangulation after the military route failed. The U.S. backed the strike. It paralyzed the industry for two months, then collapsed when loyalist workers and engineers got the pumps running again. Chavez fired thousands of executives and managers in response, stripping the internal opposition out of the state oil company in one stroke. Washington’s hand in encouraging the lockout was less visible than its role in the coup, but the strategy was the same: find whatever pressure point works and squeeze it.

And when economic conditions soured from falling oil prices and mismanagement, the U.S. found ways to make the pain worse. Hoarding and smuggling operations, along with price gouging, deepened shortages and eroded public support for Chavez. The mechanism ran through proxies and private actors rather than direct state action, but the objective matched the coup and the lockout: make the government fail in the eyes of its own people.

The Obama administration inherited this confrontational posture and tried to make it look respectable. Executive Order 13692, signed in March 2015, declared Venezuela an “unusual and extraordinary threat” to U.S. national security. The language was a legal requirement for authorizing sanctions, and it was absurd on its face. Venezuela posed no military threat to the United States. Its economy was in freefall. The declaration functioned as legal cover for targeted sanctions against individuals tied to human rights abuses and corruption. The administration sanctioned 17 officials, freezing their U.S. assets and banning their entry.

Officials called this a “smart sanctions” approach. The pitch was precision: punish regime elites, spare the general population. The pitch was deceptive. Sanctions against a state that depends on oil revenue for most of its income cascade downward through the economy no matter who the named target is. But the Obama administration kept up the pretense of surgical strikes, and in doing so built the legal architecture that the next administration would use to go much further.

The Obama administration also went after Venezuelan officials on drug trafficking charges, designating five of them as drug kingpins by 2012. Those designations gave later administrations a narrative they could expand. In June 2016, the administration assembled a 15-nation coalition in the Organization of American States to apply diplomatic pressure, though the coalition fractured before it accomplished much.

The shift in the administration’s final years was the recognition that targeted measures weren’t producing regime change. The legal groundwork for broader economic warfare was in place, even if Obama himself was reluctant to deploy it at full scale. Sanctions on PDVSA in May 2011, imposed for supplying reformate to Iran, showed a willingness to go after the oil sector. Those sanctions came off in 2016 as part of the Iran nuclear deal. But the underlying approach stayed consistent across all of it: maintain pressure while preserving enough deniability to leave the next administration room to escalate.

Washington spent the entire Chavez years convinced the regime would fold. Analysts looked at Venezuela’s poor majority and figured the enthusiasm for Bolivarian reforms would burn off once the novelty wore thin. Inside the barracks, the picture looked different. Two decades of purges and patronage had welded the officer corps to the project, and the people at Langley kept predicting a fracture that stayed hypothetical. The government learned from every failed opposition attempt, adapted, and tightened its grip. Leopoldo López’s 2014 street uprisings, the ones that killed dozens and tore up Caracas, followed the script Washington had already watched fail: U.S. officials pushed the opposition past the point of no return, and Maduro’s security forces crushed the whole thing. Chavez died in 2013. Maduro stepped into the presidency. And Washington looked back on fifteen years of bankrolling an opposition that had produced zero credible governing alternatives. The fixation on ripping Chavismo out consumed so much oxygen that nobody in the policy world paused to consider what would fill the vacuum. Policymakers assumed any replacement would be an improvement. U.S. interventions from Iraq to Libya had already stress-tested that assumption, and the results were sitting there for anyone to read.

Clinton denied Chavez a visa, and Bush backed the 2002 coup. Obama escalated in 2015, declaring Venezuela a national security threat. The faces changed. The objective stayed fixed: keep an independent leftist government from consolidating in the Western Hemisphere. Methods escalated from diplomatic snubs and covert action to targeted sanctions, but the endgame never shifted. Officials in Washington framed each new round of pressure as a response to Venezuelan provocation. But the provocations grew out of U.S. pressure in the first place. Chavez built partnerships with Cuba and Iran, then Russia, and those alliances tightened as Washington cut off normal diplomatic channels. His anti-American rhetoric tracked the pace of American subversion. The hostility fed on itself. U.S. pressure provoked the very alliances and rhetoric that Washington then cited as justification for more pressure, and policymakers refused to see their own hand in building the adversary they claimed to contain. By the second Obama term, the relationship had calcified into something neither side could walk back without losing face. Diplomacy required acknowledging mutual responsibility, and nobody in Washington was going to be the first to do that.

The National Endowment for Democracy, built in 1983 as a legal alternative to CIA covert action after the scandals of the 1970s, became the main conduit for Venezuelan operations. NED grants went to opposition parties and the journalists who covered them, with student organizations picking up what was left. The operation cultivated an opposition and branded the work as democracy promotion. USAID funneled money through “civil society” programs that bypassed the Venezuelan state, building parallel service-delivery networks that opposition actors could switch on during a crisis. The International Republican Institute and its Democratic counterpart supplied polling data and strategic communications coaching. What emerged was an opposition that depended on Washington for its survival, could theorize about winning elections, but had no organic roots and no credible plan for governing. When elections happened, opposition leaders refused to accept results and demanded U.S. intervention instead of doing the slow work of building popular support. The grift ran in a circle: American taxpayers funded opposition groups that then demanded American intervention, and the intervention justified the next round of funding.

Radio y Televisión Martí, a U.S.-funded outlet, broadcast anti-government programming across the region. American journalists built their coverage on opposition sources, presenting opposition claims as established fact while reporters ignored the government’s side. The narrative frame stayed fixed: dictator versus democracy, socialism versus freedom. The genuine redistribution Chavez’s programs achieved, and the opposition’s own record of corruption, vanished from American media accounts. Legislators and voters absorbed a one-sided story of villainy that demanded a response, and that lopsided coverage gave Washington the political cover it needed for aggressive policy. The situation in Venezuelan neighborhoods was more complicated than the coverage suggested. Venezuela under Chavez cut poverty by half and eliminated illiteracy. Cuban medical brigades staffed clinics in areas where millions of people had never seen a doctor. Oil wealth paid for all of it, and the results built a base of popular support that U.S. policymakers kept underestimating. Refusing to grant Chavismo any legitimacy meant refusing to see the political reality in front of them.

Chavez built ALBA, the Bolivarian Alliance for the Peoples of Our America, and Petrocaribe, which piped subsidized oil to Caribbean nations. Both projects chipped at U.S. economic dominance in the hemisphere. His backing of leftist governments in Bolivia, Ecuador, and Nicaragua raised the specter of an anti-American bloc. Washington treated the whole situation as zero-sum. A barrel of oil sold to China at a discount registered as a barrel stolen from U.S. markets. Cuban doctors staffing Venezuelan clinics read as communist influence spreading into the neighborhood. The Obama administration’s “pivot to Asia” did not ease anxiety about Latin American independence. If anything, it sharpened the fear of losing control over the “backyard.” U.S. policy treated Venezuelan sovereignty as an inconvenience to manage. The 2015 national security threat declaration, required by law but absurd in geopolitical terms, gave future administrations the bureaucratic trigger they needed for sanctions escalation. Obama officials expressed regret about the language after the fact. The precedent stuck, and future administrations used it.

The human cost of this confrontation started piling up well before anyone in Washington coined the phrase “maximum pressure.” The 2002 coup killed dozens. The oil lockout bled billions in lost revenue and wiped out thousands of jobs. The 2014 protests killed more people still. And through all of it, the U.S. kept insisting it supported “democratic transition,” a phrase that by then meant regime change and nothing else. You can’t promote democracy through coups and sanctions, and yet American policymakers had talked themselves into believing that Venezuelan democracy, as expressed through elections that kept returning Chavismo to power, didn’t count as the real thing. Only outcomes that suited U.S. interests qualified. That redefinition let Washington claim moral authority while violating the sovereignty of a government that voters had put there, repeatedly, in elections observers certified. The Obama administration’s targeted sanctions were less destructive than what followed, sure, but they played along with the same fiction. They punished officials for “undermining democracy” in the same breath that democratic elections had taken place.

Regime change got baked into the bureaucracy through several agencies working in parallel. The Congressional Research Service churned out regular reports framing Venezuela as a crisis demanding intervention. The State Department’s Bureau of Democracy, Human Rights, and Labor wired money to opposition activists. The Treasury Department’s Office of Foreign Assets Control built the plumbing for financial warfare. Each agency handled its slice, and the combined operation stayed consistent across election cycles in a way that made reversal hard even for a president who wanted it. Obama tried to normalize relations with Cuba, a move that might have taken some of the defensiveness out of Caracas, and the Venezuela policy apparatus kept grinding forward regardless. Congressional hawks like Marco Rubio, Ileana Ros-Lehtinen, and Bob Menendez, all representing Florida’s anti-Castro constituencies, kept the pressure constant. They understood that Venezuela served as Cuba’s financial lifeline, and that cutting it off would topple the government in Havana by proxy. That linkage explains the ferocity. U.S. policy toward Venezuela was always also a policy toward ending the Cuban Revolution.

The region pushed back, and the pushback revealed how much independence Latin American governments had accumulated. The Organization of American States, long a U.S. rubber stamp, started resisting Washington’s anti-Venezuela initiatives. In 2017, OAS Secretary General Luis Almagro, a Uruguayan diplomat with close U.S. ties, tried to invoke the Democratic Charter against Venezuela and couldn’t scrape together enough votes. The Community of Latin American and Caribbean States got created with the U.S. and Canada explicitly left out, which said plenty about where the region wanted to go. Brazil under Lula da Silva and later Dilma Rousseff kept good relations with Caracas despite U.S. pressure. Argentina under Nestor and Cristina Kirchner did the same. Colombia, under its right-wing governments, lined up with Washington every time. Without solid regional backing, unilateral action risked looking like naked aggression, and that stripped the democratic pretext of whatever cover it still had.

The economic weaponization of oil markets started before sanctions did. The U.S. encouraged Saudi Arabia to flood markets in 2014, and prices collapsed from over $100 per barrel to below $30. Venezuelan revenue cratered, and the economic crisis that sanctions would later exploit was already underway. U.S. ally states in OPEC increased production at Washington’s behest to punish both Russia and Venezuela, and the price war accomplished what direct sanctions couldn’t. It created the preconditions for humanitarian crisis while letting the U.S. keep its hands clean. Oil prices crashed, Venezuela’s dollar earnings evaporated, and the government couldn’t finance imports of food and medicine. The shortages that followed fed the popular discontent both the opposition and Washington were counting on to topple the government. The government survived anyway, which exposed the miscalculation baked into U.S. strategy. People don’t blame their own government for external economic warfare as a reflex. They rally around the flag, especially when that government can point at foreign powers and say, credibly, those people are doing this to you.

The 2015 national security threat declaration was legally necessary for sanctions, but it also shifted the rhetoric in a way that mattered. By designating Venezuela a threat, Obama handed hardliners the ammunition to argue for a complete economic embargo. The language was false. The consequences were real. It built a legal and political permission structure that the Trump administration walked straight through. Obama officials later disavowed the implications of their own order, which tells you something about how bureaucratic escalation works. You set up the machinery of coercion, and it acquires its own momentum. The “smart sanctions” were supposed to stay limited. They became the foundation for a blockade. This pattern ran through the Cold War and kept running after it. Limited measures, once everyone gets used to them, expand into comprehensive campaigns, and the alleged precision turns into collective punishment. Defenders of targeted sanctions argue they offer an alternative to war. In practice, they tend to be the prelude, creating the economic desperation and political isolation that make military intervention look like the next logical step.

The opposition’s relationship with Washington ran on a transactional logic. Leaders flew to the capital for instructions and funding. They lobbied Congress for harsher sanctions, operating on the assumption that enough economic pain would crack the government open. The moral hazard in that strategy was plain: as the humanitarian crisis deepened, opposition figures read the suffering as confirmation that collapse was around the corner. Maria Corina Machado met with Bush administration officials and took part in the 2015 “Plan Jericho” coup plot, which military intelligence foiled. Leopoldo Lopez demanded “the exit” of the government and backed the 2014 violent protests known as “La Salida.” His party, Voluntad Popular, took NED money and coordinated with U.S. embassy staff. After Venezuelan courts imprisoned Lopez for inciting violence, Washington imposed targeted sanctions on the officials who handled his prosecution, treating a domestic legal proceeding as grounds for an international response. Opposition violence went unremarked while government repression drew condemnation and sanctions. The “human rights” framing functioned as a lever for one side’s political objectives.

American outlets covered Venezuela through opposition sources and exile communities, to the near exclusion of other voices. Washington found the starvation-and-dictatorship narrative useful. It framed sanctions as humanitarian intervention and made negotiation with the government look pointless, generating the domestic political pressure that lawmakers needed to justify action. The situation on the ground was messier than the coverage suggested. Economic crisis caused real suffering, and the government kept a base of support through food distribution and subsidized housing. Community health clinics still operated in poor neighborhoods. The opposition spent its energy on international lobbying instead of building an alternative governance model at home, and that external orientation made them look like U.S. clients to a lot of Venezuelans, undercutting their domestic credibility. You could see the bind in the 2018 presidential election. Maduro won amid opposition boycotts. The opposition refused to participate and called the conditions unfair, then declared the result illegitimate. Their refusal to contest the race ceded the field. Washington recognized the illegitimacy claim as settled fact within hours, which showed that it had adopted the opposition’s strategic playbook as its own.

The legal architecture of sanctions grew more expansive with each administration. The Venezuela Defense of Human Rights and Civil Society Act of 2014, which Congress passed with bipartisan support, authorized sanctions on officials involved in human rights abuses. The law required no due process. Executive branch officials made designations by fiat, based on classified evidence, and those sanctioned had no meaningful legal recourse. The government froze their assets and published their names. International financial transactions became impossible for the designated individuals. The stigma of designation made them political pariahs, even though many were mid-level bureaucrats with no strategic importance. This process of bureaucratic punishment expanded under Trump, but Obama had normalized it. Sanctions functioned as an alternative to criminal prosecution, without trial or appeal, and that represented a departure from the rule-of-law principles Washington claimed to defend. The mechanism amounted to administrative punishment for political disagreement, with human rights language serving as the justification.

U.S. policy toward Venezuela had consequences that reached well beyond Caracas. The attempted isolation pushed Venezuela into deeper partnerships with American adversaries. Russia extended loans and weapons, and provided diplomatic cover at the UN Security Council. China lent billions, receiving oil shipments in return and gaining a foothold in Latin American energy markets. Iran established direct flights and trade relationships, exchanging technical assistance for Venezuelan support. The anti-U.S. bloc that resulted was the exact outcome American policy was supposed to prevent. Washington’s pressure to isolate Venezuela pushed Caracas toward the foreign allies that the Monroe Doctrine, invoked in internal policy documents across multiple administrations, was meant to keep out. The resulting axis of resistance, while economically limited, provided enough support to keep the Maduro government afloat despite sanctions. The episode showed the limits of U.S. coercive power in a multipolar world. China and Russia had the capacity and willingness to absorb some costs of defiance, and that meant the U.S. lacked the power to impose its will alone.

The economic damage from pre-sanctions pressure was severe. The 2014 oil price collapse, induced by Saudi overproduction at U.S. urging, cut Venezuelan government revenue by more than 50 percent in a single year. That fiscal shock made it impossible to maintain import levels, and the resulting shortages were real and politically damaging. But other oil producers, Russia among them, weathered the same price shock without humanitarian catastrophe. Low prices combined with escalating sanctions to make Venezuela’s crisis catastrophic. Sanctions prevented borrowing and blocked asset sales, cutting off access to financial markets at the moment Venezuela needed liquidity most. The trap had a specific sequence: create economic distress through oil markets, then impose sanctions that prevent any response. Washington could claim that sanctions responded to government misrule, but the sequencing told a different story. The economic crisis preceded the sanctions. The sanctions made it existential.

The opposition kept losing, and the losses kept looking the same. The 2002 coup had no popular base behind it, and the military support it counted on turned out to be a few generals willing to make a phone call, not a chain of command willing to hold. A year later, the oil lockout fell apart because the rank-and-file workers at PDVSA refused to walk off the job for a cause that didn’t include them. You can skip ahead to 2014, where the protests drew middle-class university students in Caracas and very few factory workers in Valencia or Maracaibo, and then to 2019, where Guaidó stood on a overpass waiting for a military defection that the generals had no intention of delivering. Four attempts, four structural reasons for failure, and the response each time was to try the same playbook with a louder megaphone. Every new opposition leader told Washington that mass defection was weeks away, that the regime was hollow, that the next push would be the one that broke it open. And Washington kept writing the checks, because the alternative meant sitting down and admitting that Chavismo held durable support among the people the U.S. claimed to be liberating. Admitting that meant admitting U.S. policy was imposing a minority preference on a country that hadn’t asked for it. Nobody in the State Department or the NSC wanted to write that memo. So the same assumptions went into the next initiative, and the next initiative produced the same wreckage.

Florida made all of this harder to unwind. The Cuban-American and Venezuelan exile communities in Miami concentrate in a swing state, organize their PACs with a discipline that most domestic lobbies can’t match, and vote in numbers that make every presidential candidate do the math on 29 electoral votes before touching Venezuela policy. These communities carry real trauma from the governments they fled, and that trauma translated into a demand for maximalist positions: full regime change, no negotiation, no recognition of Maduro’s legitimacy under any configuration. Their PACs funded congressional candidates who ran on hardline platforms. Their Spanish-language media outlets in South Florida amplified every opposition narrative at full volume while giving the Maduro government’s perspective zero airtime. Their lobbying shops in Washington drafted the actual legislative language for sanctions bills. The effect was a domestic constraint that made any compromise politically suicidal. A president who floated even limited engagement with Caracas would face ads calling him soft on dictatorship before the press conference ended. Trump’s maximum-pressure posture was, in part, a calculation aimed at Florida’s electorate. Biden tried a more calibrated approach and got burned for it, so he kept most sanctions in place while attempting narrow diplomatic channels that went nowhere. The policy that emerged owed more to a constituency with veto power than to any strategic assessment of what might work.

The legal architecture around all this is thin, and everyone involved knows it. The UN Charter prohibits unilateral coercive measures that interfere with sovereign equality, and the U.S. imposed what functions as a financial blockade on Venezuela without Security Council authorization, using its control over dollar clearing and SWIFT access as the enforcement mechanism. The sanctions hit proportionality problems because they punish 28 million people for the alleged crimes of a governing clique. They hit necessity problems because Washington chose the most restrictive measures available rather than targeted ones aimed at specific officials. They hit sovereignty problems because the stated goal was to dictate who governs Venezuela and under what constitutional arrangement. And there’s no court that can do anything about it. The ICC has no jurisdiction over economic sanctions. The Security Council can’t act because the U.S. holds a veto. The WTO’s dispute mechanism can’t adjudicate national-security exceptions, which is the loophole Washington drives a truck through. Venezuela filed cases at the International Court of Justice, but the ICJ has no enforcement arm, so the rulings sit in a drawer. The practical result is that a country with reserve-currency dominance can strangle another economy through financial channels, and the targeted country’s only real defense is to leave the dollar system. Venezuela has done this partially, settling oil trades with China and Russia in yuan and rubles, which reduces the leverage but doesn’t restore what was lost.

The Center for Economic and Policy Research estimated that sanctions killed roughly 40,000 people in Venezuela between 2017 and 2020, mostly by choking off imports of insulin, antiretrovirals, chemotherapy drugs, and replacement parts for dialysis machines. Alena Douhan, the UN Special Rapporteur on unilateral coercive measures, documented catastrophic shortages of food and medicine and the degradation of water treatment and electrical infrastructure that the sanctions made impossible to maintain. The 35 percent GDP contraction in 2019, tied directly to the oil sanctions that cut Venezuela’s export revenue, ranks among the worst peacetime economic collapses recorded in the modern era. The architects of maximum pressure understood this would happen. The theory, stated openly in policy circles, was that economic desperation would force either a popular uprising or a military coup, and the suffering was the lever you pulled to get there. What the lever produced instead was seven million people leaving the country and the rest going quiet, too exhausted to protest. The deaths got entered into the policy calculus as a sign that pressure needed to increase, that the regime was feeling the squeeze, that one more round of sanctions would tip the balance. The people the policy claimed to help were the ones paying for it in body bags and bus tickets to Bogotá.

Maduro took his second inauguration in January 2019, and the Trump administration treated it as the opening it had been waiting for. The opposition-controlled National Assembly named Juan Guaidó, a 35-year-old legislator most Venezuelans couldn’t have picked out of a lineup, as interim president. The U.S. recognized him within hours. A coordinated recognition campaign followed, pulling in over 50 nations. Guaidó went from anonymous assembly member to receiving standing ovations in the European Parliament and the U.S. Congress inside a single news cycle, promising a transition that would take weeks. Washington froze $7 billion in Venezuelan government assets, including every PDVSA holding on U.S. soil, and imposed secondary sanctions that threatened any foreign company doing business with the Maduro government. The plan was to recognize a parallel government, seize the state’s overseas assets, cut off all revenue streams, and wait for the structure to buckle. The military stayed in its barracks. The government kept functioning. Guaidó’s actual support inside Venezuela, measured by turnout at his rallies and polling in the barrios, turned out to be a fraction of what his international press coverage suggested. By 2022, the opposition coalition itself stripped him of the interim title. The whole episode demonstrated that external recognition, without a base inside the country willing to act on it, is a press release with a flag pin on the lapel.

### **Part Two: Maximum Pressure and Systemic Collapse (2017-2021)**

The Trump administration dropped the surgical-precision language Obama’s team had favored and went after the Venezuelan economy as a whole. They called it “maximum pressure,” codified it through a stack of executive orders, and aimed at something bigger than punishing any individual official. The target was the country’s ability to function. The phrase they used internally, according to people who sat in those meetings, was to make Venezuela “scream,” which is the same language Henry Kissinger used about Chile in 1973. Executive Order 13808, signed in August 2017, barred U.S. persons from touching new debt issued by PDVSA or the Venezuelan government. That single order locked Venezuela out of U.S. capital markets and choked off the borrowing it needed to finance imports. E.O. 13827 went after the petro cryptocurrency, the government’s attempt to route around the sanctions. E.O. 13835 blocked purchase of any debt owed to the Venezuelan government, closing off asset sales that might have generated some liquidity. The architects of this sequence wanted a liquidity crisis that would grind the economy down over months, giving the opposition time to organize before the pain became a full humanitarian catastrophe.

E.O. 13850, signed in November 2018, handed Treasury the authority to sanction entire industries rather than picking off individuals one at a time. Then came January 28, 2019, and the designation of PDVSA itself as a sanctioned entity. Treasury blocked all PDVSA property and interests under U.S. jurisdiction, barred U.S. persons from transacting with the company, and seized Citgo, PDVSA’s U.S. subsidiary, in the process. That one action cut off roughly 95 percent of Venezuela’s foreign exchange earnings. The government lost its primary source of dollars, and with it the ability to pay for imports. Officials in Washington justified the seizure by claiming it prevented the “illegitimate Maduro regime” from plundering assets. The claim didn’t survive contact with what happened. Washington confiscated Venezuelan assets and transferred control to the United States. The Venezuelan people got nothing. April 2019 brought sanctions on Venezuela’s Central Bank, closing the last channel for international financial transactions. E.O. 13884, in August 2019, imposed a complete economic embargo, blocking all property of the entire Maduro government. Financial strangulation, at that point, was complete.

You can track the damage in the numbers, and they’re ugly. Between 2009 and May 2021, the U.S. imposed 431 Venezuela-related designations. The Trump administration accounted for 148 of those under E.O. 13850 in 2019 alone. Sanctioned individuals went from 17 under Obama to 135 under Trump. Oil export revenue fell from $4.826 billion in 2018 to $477 million in 2020. Production dropped from 3.2 million barrels per day in the 1990s to 558,000 barrels per day in 2021. GDP contracted 35 percent in 2019. Hyperinflation peaked at 10 million percent annually. These were the direct, intended results of the policy. The sanctions created a self-reinforcing cycle where falling production reduced revenue, which prevented maintenance investment, which caused further production declines. Washington then pointed to the collapse as evidence of Maduro’s mismanagement and used that evidence to justify the next round of sanctions. The victim got blamed for the wounds the attacker inflicted.

The humanitarian damage started fast. Sanctions blocked Venezuela from purchasing food and medicine. Pharmaceutical companies feared violating sanctions rules, so they pulled out. Banks refused to process payments even for goods that technically fell under humanitarian exemptions. The COVID-19 pandemic made everything worse. Venezuela couldn’t access vaccines, test kits, or protective equipment through normal channels, and the government turned to Russia and China for assistance, which Washington then cited as evidence of dangerous alliances. Sanctions advocates pointed to humanitarian exemptions in the regulations. Those exemptions were complex and narrow by design, and the practical effect was a near-total blockade of essential goods. Former UN Rapporteur Douhan documented how sanctions prevented maintenance of water and electricity infrastructure, triggering secondary health crises on top of the primary ones. Malnutrition rates climbed. Measles and diphtheria, diseases Venezuela had previously eradicated, came back. The sanctions killed through systematic deprivation, slow and total, and the body count was no smaller for the absence of bombs.

On January 23, 2019, Guaidó declared himself president in a Caracas plaza, and Trump recognized him within hours. The U.S. let Guaidó appoint “ambassadors” who gained access to Venezuelan state assets abroad. He named boards of directors for PDVSA’s foreign subsidiaries, Citgo included. These parallel institutions existed on paper to provide legal cover for asset seizures. The whole arrangement was a bureaucratic fiction, a government that operated out of Washington and Miami while having no presence in Caracas. Guaidó’s outfit couldn’t pay soldiers, issue passports, or collect taxes. It was a Potemkin regime, and its sole function was to make the sanctions and asset confiscation look lawful. The April 30, 2019 uprising failed because the military didn’t defect. Soldiers stayed in their posts. The government didn’t fall. Guaidó faded into irrelevance after that, though the U.S. kept recognizing him until 2023, which tells you something about how much the fiction mattered to the people running it.

Washington’s regional coalition proved hollow too. The Lima Group, formed in 2017 to coordinate pressure on Venezuela, pulled in most South American nations plus Canada. But governments changed, and support bled out. Mexico walked after Andrés Manuel López Obrador won in 2018, and Argentina flipped once Alberto Fernández replaced Mauricio Macri. Brazil’s commitment went wobbly after Jair Bolsonaro lost, and by 2022 the Lima Group had dissolved in everything but name. The Inter-American Treaty of Reciprocal Assistance, invoked in September 2019 to authorize collective action, produced zero military participation. Colombia, the only member sharing a border with Venezuela, refused to let anyone use its territory for an invasion. The regional consensus Washington kept pointing to fell apart. Most Latin American governments opposed military intervention and questioned whether sanctions worked at all. They absorbed the refugee flows and the economic disruption while the U.S. dictated policy from a few thousand miles away, and the campaign isolated Washington while Caracas stayed untouched.

Military threats escalated through 2019 and 2020. Trump told the press that “all options are on the table,” a phrase that included military invasion. He pressed advisers on whether a naval blockade was feasible. The administration dispatched the Navy hospital ship USNS Comfort to the Caribbean, officially to treat Venezuelan refugees, though the timing and positioning read as a show of force. National Security Advisor John Bolton let a photographer catch his notepad reading “5,000 troops to Colombia,” a leak meant to rattle Caracas. The administration discussed a “decapitation strike” against Maduro and other senior officials. The threats carried weight. In 2020, Venezuelan forces intercepted and defeated “Operation Gideon,” an amphibious incursion by Venezuelan dissidents and American contractors. Two former U.S. special forces soldiers ended up in Venezuelan custody, exposing direct American involvement in paramilitary operations. Whoever planned the operation botched it so thoroughly that failure looked intentional, as though the whole thing existed to provide a pretext for further escalation. The administration had erased any remaining boundary between covert action and overt military pressure.

The economic theory behind maximum pressure was broken from the start. Policymakers assumed that Venezuelan elites, staring at personal financial ruin, would turn on Maduro, and that the military, watching its institutional resources evaporate, would stage a coup. They also bet the population, ground down by shortages, would rise up. Those assumptions ignored how sanctions function in practice. Sanctions create a siege mentality that rallies regime supporters against the foreign aggressor, and they wipe out the middle class and business opposition who might otherwise push for change, since their livelihoods get eliminated alongside everyone else’s. On top of that, sanctions make regime insiders more dependent on the government, because only state connections still provide access to scarce resources. Sanctions solidified the regime and eliminated alternatives. A general or minister weighing defection had nowhere to go, because the U.S. had sanctioned all possible receptor institutions. Staying loyal was the only rational choice left.

The legal contortions the administration needed to keep the sanctions regime running grew absurd. The Treasury Department’s Office of Foreign Assets Control put out hundreds of pages of guidance, general licenses, and frequently asked questions trying to spell out what counted as permitted activity. Humanitarian organizations complained that the rules were so tangled they didn’t dare operate in Venezuela for fear of an inadvertent violation. Banks engaged in “overcompliance,” refusing all transactions touching Venezuelan entities rather than risk fines. Policymakers turned that legal uncertainty into a weapon. The U.S. government could claim, on paper, that food and medicine were exempt, while knowing the complexity of the rules prevented their delivery. The whole apparatus functioned as lawfare, legal process pressed into service as a tool of war. The victims were sick children and pregnant women who couldn’t obtain insulin or antibiotics, and elderly patients who went without chemotherapy drugs.

The COVID-19 pandemic stripped away whatever policy rationale remained for maximum pressure. The virus tore through Venezuela, and the U.S. blocked vaccine purchases through COVAX by refusing to release frozen funds. Maduro’s government eventually obtained vaccines from Russia and China, and Washington condemned the arrangement as “vaccine diplomacy” from adversaries. Washington had prevented Venezuela from accessing Western vaccines, then attacked Maduro for accepting Eastern ones. The sanctions had become an end in themselves, detached from any policy objective. Regime change had dropped off the agenda, and punishment remained. The policy had become a tantrum. Venezuela refused to submit, so Washington would make it suffer. That the suffering achieved no strategic purpose didn’t register with anyone making the decisions, and the people running the policy didn’t appear to need a reason beyond the punishment itself.

Venezuela’s government kept trying to find ways around the sanctions, and Washington kept closing them. The state gold mining company Minerven started financing imports through gold reserves, so the Treasury sanctioned Minerven. Oil sales through intermediaries drew secondary sanctions on the trading companies involved. The petro cryptocurrency and the digital exchanges handling it got banned when Caracas tried routing transactions through blockchain. Every workaround produced a new prohibition within months. You strangle someone and then make it illegal for them to breathe through a tube. And the metrics that told you the policy was “working” had nothing to do with whether Venezuelans got a say in their own governance. Oil production fell. GDP contracted. Poverty climbed. Those were the numbers on the scoreboard.

The opposition treated sanctions as a substitute for politics. Guaidó’s envoys in Washington lobbied for tougher measures because they’d calculated that economic collapse would hand them power on a tray, sparing them the work of building actual popular support through governance proposals. Political strip-mining is the right phrase for it: you extract authority from the ruins of an economy you helped wreck, and you call the extraction a mandate. And when the regime didn’t change, when Maduro stayed put, the opposition blamed insufficient pressure. They called for military intervention. They had no plan for rebuilding the economy they’d spent years helping to annihilate, no vision past the single sentence “Maduro must go,” which U.S. recognition then inflated into a pretended presidency. Opportunism, plain and simple. And it produced nothing.

The fallout spread well past Venezuela’s borders. Russia parked nuclear-capable bombers in the Caribbean and stationed a permanent advisory mission there, a direct answer to U.S. threats against Caracas. China extended a $60-billion loan-for-oil program that made Venezuela dependent on Beijing while handing China a strategic energy reserve sitting in the Western Hemisphere. Iran shipped refined oil products to Venezuela in open defiance of U.S. sanctions, building a sanctions-busting arrangement that other targeted states could copy. Washington expelled Venezuela from its own sphere of influence and shoved it straight into the arms of every rival power it claimed to be containing. The Monroe Doctrine was supposed to keep foreign powers out of the hemisphere. The sanctions regime created a vacuum that Russia, China, and Iran walked right into. You can punish a country into poverty and still lose the strategic contest you started the punishment to win, and that’s what happened here. Sanctions, in a world where Caracas can call Moscow or Beijing or Tehran, don’t enforce hegemony. They enforce suffering.

Inside Venezuela, the government stopped trying to maintain the consumer economy it had run before the pressure campaign. It pivoted to keeping people fed and minimally healthy: subsidized food boxes through the CLAP program, community health clinics, direct cash transfers to the poorest households. The military got control of key import sectors, which guaranteed loyalty and also opened wide corridors for corruption. The state grew more authoritarian as it fought to survive. It didn’t collapse. The Bolivarian project, stripped of its grand developmental ambitions, shrank to the business of keeping a population alive. Washington called that a failure. A government adapting its entire economic model under the most severe financial pressure the hemisphere has seen in decades, keeping a sovereign state intact while its currency and its oil sector get dismantled from the outside, is a government doing what governments do under siege. Nobody in Caracas was going to build a prosperous social democracy while the Treasury Department froze every dollar it could reach. The relevant question was whether Venezuela would remain a sovereign state. It did.

Guaidó’s parallel government operated out of Miami and Madrid, and its main function was seizing assets. His appointees took control of Venezuelan diplomatic properties and bank accounts, then sold off holdings like the Monómeros fertilizer plant in Colombia, allegedly pocketing the proceeds. They issued bonds supposedly backed by future oil production, though the legality and value of those instruments were dubious at best. The U.S. government granted legal immunity to Guaidó’s representatives for actions that would have constituted fraud under any other circumstances, on the reasoning that these were the legitimate acts of a legitimate government. A government that can’t set foot in its own territory is a legal fiction maintained for the convenience of asset transfers. And when the fiction finally collapsed, the stolen assets didn’t flow back to Venezuela. They stayed frozen, or they vanished into private accounts. The theft stuck.

The Trump administration built the legal scaffolding for military intervention across these years. In March 2020, the Justice Department indicted Maduro and 14 officials on narco-terrorism charges, alleging they conspired with the FARC to traffic cocaine into the U.S. The indictment wrapped a sitting head of state in a war-crimes narrative, and the department posted a $15 million reward for Maduro’s capture. Regime change by legal accusation. Convicted traffickers and former Venezuelan officials seeking leniency supplied the testimony behind the charges, and the prosecution never brought evidence into open court. The indictments did two things at once: they stripped Maduro’s legitimacy on the international stage, and they gave any future administration a ready-made legal pretext for military action dressed as criminal apprehension. The administration discussed military options openly, including a direct invasion. Elliott Abrams, the architect of Reagan-era Central American interventions, got the special envoy post, which telegraphed a return to 1980s-style covert warfare. Officials considered a naval blockade and rejected it, though they deployed naval assets to the Caribbean for “counter-narcotics” missions that targeted Venezuela. By that point, the line between law enforcement and war had gone blurry enough that the distinction stopped mattering in practice.

Maximum pressure failed at everything it set out to do. Maduro kept his seat. The generals kept their posts. And the policy went on destroying the economy, killing tens of thousands, displacing millions, and handing the regime a nationalist narrative it could not have scripted on its own. The only thing the strategy produced was suffering, and after a while the suffering stopped looking like a side effect and started looking like the deliverable. The obsession with regime change consumed the entire policy apparatus. Officials judged sanctions by their severity, by how much they hurt, and stopped asking whether they worked. Harder meant better. Trump officials boasted about the destructiveness of their measures and treated economic ruin as proof of commitment. The whole operation ran as performance for domestic political consumption. Venezuelan people became props in an American political drama, their hunger and displacement serving as evidence of Washington’s resolve. And when the policy failed, officials blamed insufficient cruelty. The strategy was sound. They hadn’t hurt people enough yet. This logic has no limiting principle, and the next step down the road is military force.

Biden’s team took over in 2021 and recalibrated while keeping the sanctions architecture intact. They recognized that maximum pressure had failed, but they held onto the existing measures as leverage. The approach got the label “calibrated pragmatism”: maintain pressure, offer limited relief in exchange for political concessions. This acknowledged a reality the Trump officials had refused to entertain. The Maduro government was not collapsing. You had to negotiate with it. The Barbados Agreement of October 2023 put the approach into practice. The U.S. temporarily lifted oil, gas, and gold sanctions in exchange for commitments to release political prisoners and hold free elections in 2024. The administration pulled back tactically while keeping the strategic goal fixed. Regime change stayed on the table. The method shifted to negotiation.

Internal debates under Biden revealed that the confusion persisted. A draft Treasury Department review from 2021, never released, reportedly proposed systemic reform of sanctions policy and acknowledged their humanitarian costs alongside their limited effectiveness. Then Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, and that shelved any reconsideration. Sanctions against Russia became the priority, and admitting that sanctions on Venezuela had failed would have undercut the credibility of the Russia campaign. So the administration kept enforcing measures it privately recognized as counterproductive. Policy inertia, driven by geopolitical convenience. The human cost in Venezuela ranked below the messaging requirements of the Ukraine war, and the sanctions regime, having outlived its original purpose, persisted as bureaucratic default. Nobody in the building had an incentive to touch it.

The relief Biden offered came with tight restrictions bolted on. General License 41B let Chevron resume limited operations, but only to recoup existing debts, with no expansion of production. General License 40C permitted LPG exports for domestic cooking fuel, addressing one specific humanitarian need while withholding general relief. The administration managed the sanctions rather than removing them, doling out narrow exceptions and keeping the power to yank them back. It exercised that power in April 2024, when Maduro barred Maria Corina Machado from the ballot and the relief evaporated. The sequence ran the same way each cycle: offer relief, demand compliance, pull it back when Maduro disappoints you. Venezuela got enough economic air to keep from suffocating entirely, and the administration withheld enough to prevent recovery. The policy kept the patient alive and crippled, on a ventilator that Washington could switch off whenever it felt like making a point.

The second Trump administration came back in 2025 and went straight to maximum pressure, except this time the pressure had a military edge welded onto it. March 2025 brought a tariff proclamation that penalized any country still buying Venezuelan oil, which was the administration trying to enforce secondary sanctions through trade policy because the Treasury route had gotten cumbersome. Tren de Aragua got designated a Foreign Terrorist Organization. The Cartel de los Soles got tagged as Specially Designated Global Terrorists. Both designations handed the executive branch legal authorities it didn’t previously hold for putting troops or drones in the air. The bounty on Maduro’s head went from $25 million to $50 million. All of that was escalation along lines already drawn. The thing that broke the pattern came on October 15, 2025, when the administration publicly acknowledged that Trump had signed a presidential finding authorizing lethal CIA operations inside Venezuela, with the stated objective of removing Maduro from power. You read that correctly. A president looked into a microphone and described his own regime-change authorization. The deniability that had papered over covert action for seventy years was gone, and nobody in the White House seemed bothered by the loss.

September 2025 is when the shooting started. U.S. forces struck vessels in the Caribbean and killed at least 37 people, the first time American military power had been turned directly on targets tied to Venezuela. The administration called these counter-narcotics operations under the banner “Operation Money Badger,” and maybe some of the boats were carrying product, but the timing and the choice of targets sent a message that had nothing to do with interdiction. Three amphibious vessels carrying 2,200 Marines moved into the region. B-52 bombers flew routes that skimmed Venezuelan airspace. The Gerald R. Ford Carrier Strike Group parked itself within striking distance. You don’t stage that hardware for a drug bust. The legal team’s workaround was classifying the situation as a “non-international armed conflict” against designated terrorist organizations, a label that let the administration sidestep congressional war powers entirely. The strikes ran roughshod over sovereignty and proportionality as the UN Charter defines them. They were acts of aggression. The administration called them law enforcement, and that rhetorical swap let officials stand at podiums claiming compliance with international norms while the norms were being shredded in the water below.

You can trace the strategic thinking behind all this, and it traces back to a dead end. Twenty years of sanctions and covert operations had produced nothing. The opposition was discredited and fractured. Maduro was still in the palace. For hardliners, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio chief among them, military force became the last drawer left to open. The 2025 National Security Strategy dragged the Monroe Doctrine back into explicit policy language and framed Venezuela as a central threat to hemispheric security. The document claimed to ditch democracy promotion in favor of pragmatic security interests, and then in the same breath affirmed a regime-change orientation toward Caracas. The way officials squared that circle was by defining Venezuelan sovereignty itself as the threat. A socialist government, by the fact of existing, violated the Monroe Doctrine’s old principle that the Western Hemisphere must remain “master in its own house,” which has always meant master in Washington’s house. The doctrine was written for U.S. mastery. The 2025 version stopped pretending otherwise.

The risks here are the kind that don’t require a war college to identify. Venezuela’s terrain is mountainous and broken, the civilian population is heavily armed, and urban combat in Caracas would produce casualties at a scale Latin America hasn’t seen in its recent wars. Colombian guerrilla groups already operating along the border would get pulled in, and you’d have a multi-sided civil war within weeks. Russian and Chinese personnel in Venezuela are limited in number, but even a handful creates the possibility of a great-power incident that nobody in Washington has gamed out to its conclusion. And the assumption that Venezuelans would greet U.S. troops with relief is a fantasy that ignores two decades of sanctions-driven resentment. Anti-American feeling is already high enough to make any occupation politically unworkable from day one. The RAND Corporation ran the numbers and warned that intervention would be long, bloody, and nearly impossible to exit. Iraq would look like a rehearsal by comparison. The policy amounted to a gamble on the impossible: squeeze hard enough to force collapse without starting a war, while staging the forces to fight the war if collapse didn’t come. The logic fed itself in a loop. Sanctions failed, so that proved the need for war. War was unthinkable, so that proved the need for more sanctions. There was no off-ramp, and the people designing the policy seemed to know it and proceed anyway.

The humanitarian damage from direct military action would make the sanctions catastrophe look like a preliminary round. Strikes on Venezuelan infrastructure would take out what’s left of the electrical grid, the water systems, the hospitals that are still somehow functioning. The refugee flow, already past 7 million people, would multiply, and Colombia, which has absorbed the largest share so far and is buckling under it, could break. The U.S. southern border would face a surge with no precedent in the hemisphere’s history. Oil prices would spike. Regional allies who’ve been quietly cooperating would find their governments destabilized overnight. Whatever credibility the United States still held as a hemispheric leader would be spent in a single news cycle. Advocates for intervention waved all of this away by insisting the status quo was intolerable, that something had to be done, even if the something they proposed would make every metric worse than the disease. The need to act had swallowed the calculation of what acting would cost, and the people who’d be paying that cost were 2,000 miles south, in a country they’d never visited, making the decision for them.

The U.S. government constructed the narcoterrorism narrative to justify military action, and the foundations were shaky from the start. The Maduro indictment leaned on testimony from former Venezuelan officials who were themselves facing U.S. criminal charges. Calling the Cartel de los Soles, a loose network of military officers involved in drug trafficking, a “terrorist organization” required the Justice Department to stretch its own definitions past the breaking point. Some Venezuelan officials have trafficked drugs. Officials in plenty of other countries have too, including countries the U.S. calls allies. The specific claim that Maduro personally directed a conspiracy to flood the U.S. with cocaine doesn’t hold up against the available evidence. Cocaine transiting Venezuela has declined in recent years, and most of the traffic now moves through Central America and Mexico. The terrorist designation was a legal fiction that the administration manufactured to clear a path for military force. It was the WMD charge of the Venezuela conflict, a scary story assembled and repeated until war felt like the only responsible choice.

Brazil, Colombia, Argentina, Mexico, and Chile all raised alarms about the 2025 U.S. military buildup. These governments watched what happened in Central America and the Caribbean during earlier interventions, and they had no interest in getting pulled into a conflict they didn’t start. López Obrador offered to mediate, insisting that dialogue was the only path out. Petro, the Colombian president and a former guerrilla himself, warned that military action would tear the Andean region apart. Lula called for respect for Venezuelan sovereignty. Even right-wing governments in Ecuador and Uruguay pulled back from Washington’s position. The hemispheric unity the administration kept referencing didn’t exist in practice. The nations the Monroe Doctrine supposedly protected rejected it outright. The country ending up alone was the United States.

The economic toolkit Washington deployed against Venezuela didn’t stay in Venezuela. Secondary sanctions and asset seizures, paired with the recognition of a parallel government, became standard moves in U.S. statecraft after that. The administration tested these tools on a weak target before turning them on Russia and China. Venezuela served as the proving ground for 21st-century economic warfare. The “maximum pressure” campaign never produced regime change, but it proved the U.S. could wreck a medium-sized economy through financial channels alone. That proof became the architecture for the Russia sanctions imposed after 2022. Washington treated the human cost in Venezuela as acceptable collateral damage in a larger project of refining sanctions as a weapon. The campaign failed at its stated political goal. It succeeded as a technical demonstration, and that’s the reason it keeps getting replicated.

### **Part Three: Strategic Fiasco and the Future of Coercion (2021-2025)**

Biden’s team walked into a policy machine that had broken down years ago and that nobody in either party wanted to be the one to switch off. Two decades of coercion had produced no regime change. What those two decades had produced was a humanitarian catastrophe, and the administration’s answer to that fact was a phrase: “calibrated pragmatism.” The sanctions architecture stayed intact. All 431 designations from 2009 through 2021 remained in force, and the $7 billion in frozen Venezuelan assets stayed locked in Treasury accounts. The parallel government charade got abandoned in 2023, though by then it had already done its job of providing legal cover for asset seizures. The only thing that shifted was the language around the policy, plus a narrow, conditional sliver of relief.

The Barbados Agreement of October 2023 was the closest this approach got to looking like diplomacy. U.S. officials negotiated it in secret with Maduro’s people and opposition representatives, and the deal on paper looked substantial: temporary lifting of oil, gas, and gold sanctions in exchange for the release of political prisoners, the reinstatement of banned opposition candidates, and internationally monitored elections in 2024. Treasury issued General License 44, which let PDVSA sell oil on international markets for six months. Repsol and Eni, the European companies with the most exposure, got specific licenses to restart operations and claw back unpaid debts through oil shipments. The structure was coercive diplomacy in its pure form. You dangle enough relief to make the other side dependent on the next tranche, and you keep your hand on the valve the whole time.

The six-month expiration date gave the game away. The relief existed to create a dependency that Washington could yank back the moment Maduro stepped out of line, and the narrow window made compliance with the full list of demands politically impossible for him. The Machado requirement was the poison pill everyone at the table understood it to be. Machado, a hardline opposition figure who had backed the 2002 coup in public and kept close ties to U.S. officials, faced a ban on holding office tied to corruption investigations and her acceptance of foreign funding for anti-government activities. Venezuela’s constitution prohibits candidates who take foreign money, the same rule you’ll find in most democracies. Washington called the ban a sign of dictatorship. The U.S. demand, in practice, was that Venezuela suspend its own constitutional provisions to make room for an American-backed candidate. The Barbados table was an ultimatum with a negotiating format bolted onto it.

Maduro’s government refused to let Machado run, and Washington seized the pretext. In April 2024, Treasury revoked General License 44 and snapped the oil blockade back into place. The administration threatened secondary sanctions against any country that bought Venezuelan crude, which meant compliance with U.S. political demands was the only route to keeping the economy on life support. The Venezuelan opposition, operating through the Unitary Platform, had signed onto this arrangement, and that choice exposed how thin its own political base ran. Instead of building a movement that could win an election on its own merits, the opposition leaned on U.S. economic pressure to remove stronger competitors from the ballot. The strategy treated Venezuelan voters as an afterthought to a process managed out of Washington.

Leaked documents and anonymous statements from administration officials showed a team arguing with itself. A 40-page Treasury Department draft review from 2021, one that the administration kept internal, acknowledged that the sanctions had missed their political targets while inflicting massive humanitarian harm. The document proposed structural changes: clearer humanitarian exemptions and sunset clauses that would force a periodic reassessment of whether the sanctions were doing anything at all. Then Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, and the review went into a drawer. The administration could hardly concede that its Venezuela sanctions were counterproductive while telling the world its Russia sanctions were indispensable. The political need to treat sanctions as a working tool overrode what the administration’s own analysts had documented: that these measures devastate civilian populations and leave the targeted regimes sitting where they were.

The war in Ukraine broke the old calculus. A global energy crisis sent Western markets scrambling for Russian alternatives, which made Venezuelan oil attractive again. Maduro regained influence he lacked since 2014. Energy hawks pressured the administration to lift sanctions and boost global supply, while Florida politicians demanded maximum pressure. The administration produced the Barbados Agreement, a conditional relief package that frustrated both sides. Energy companies desperate for Venezuelan reserves lobbied Washington to erase the sanctions. Chevron pumped oil under General License 41B, proving limited engagement yielded barrels while preserving the political status quo. The Democratic Party relied on Florida’s electoral votes and feared angering the Cuban-American lobby, so the party avoided a total reset.

Caracas understood the trap and played the Barbados Agreement to its advantage. The government released a few political prisoners and allowed limited opposition participation, and kept tight control over the electoral authority while maintaining alliances with Russia and China. Officials used the six-month relief period to sign new oil deals with Chinese firms and deepen military ties with Iran. They built alternative financial mechanisms outside the dollar system. Washington reimposed sanctions, and Venezuela stood ready because officials had diversified their trade relationships and proved Caracas could survive brief windows of relief. Washington wanted to prove sanctions relief required total surrender. Caracas wanted to prove sanctions were survivable and relief demanded too much sovereignty. Both sides made their case.

The opposition wasted the Barbados Agreement. Officials declared María Corina Machado ineligible, and she demanded Washington reimpose sanctions, prioritizing American policy preferences over building a viable electoral coalition. Washington funded the opposition for two decades, breeding a split between factions willing to negotiate with Maduro and hardliners demanding maximalist confrontation. The group operated as a bureaucracy of protest, relying on external funding and recognition, and failed to develop local political strength. Opposition candidates competed on a level playing field in the 2024 regional elections and struggled, winning traditional strongholds of the urban middle class and losing the rest of the country. Food subsidies sustained the Chavista base, and voters remained hostile to foreign interference.

Trump took office in January 2025 and revived hardline coercion. His administration renewed designations for Venezuelan officials and expanded terrorism authorities while issuing explicit military threats. Washington issued a March 2025 executive order imposing tariffs on countries importing Venezuelan oil, creating a new pressure tool. The administration decided to punish any nation trading with Venezuela, expanding the pressure past Caracas. This secondary tariff regime carried dubious legal standing and risked WTO violations. It signaled absolute hostility. The administration pursued total isolation, making the 2019 “maximum pressure” campaign look moderate by comparison.

Washington released the 2025 National Security Strategy document in September and made the Monroe Doctrine explicit for the first time since the Cold War. The text stated “the Western Hemisphere must remain master in its own house,” a direct quote from Kennedy-era documents. The authors targeted Venezuela and its allies in Cuba and Nicaragua, labeling them “destabilizing autocracies” threatening U.S. security through mass migration and transnational crime. The strategy claimed to abandon “democracy promotion” as a primary objective and focus on “core national interests.” This was semantic trickery. Washington spent decades supporting dictatorships, which made the democracy promotion rhetoric hypocritical. The new language was honest, framing U.S. primacy as the goal and democracy as the pretext. Washington abandoned the pretext while retaining the goal.

In 2025, the U.S. government designated the Tren de Aragua gang as a Foreign Terrorist Organization and the Cartel de los Soles as Specially Designated Global Terrorists to give the military new legal authorities. Lawyers argued about the legality for months. Tren de Aragua started as a Venezuelan prison gang and grew into a transnational criminal organization running extortion and human smuggling. The gang operates for profit. The FTO designation stretched the definition of terrorism past its usual focus on political violence. The Cartel de los Soles designation named Maduro himself as the leader, so the United States declared the sitting president of a sovereign state the head of a terrorist group. The administration waged legal warfare, using domestic U.S. law to criminalize foreign governance.

Trump removed all ambiguity on October 15, 2025, by acknowledging he had authorized lethal CIA operations in Venezuela. Presidential findings for covert action stay classified. Announcing them serves as psychological warfare meant to intimidate targets and demonstrate resolve. The authorization permitted paramilitary raids and direct action against Venezuelan officials. The CIA left clear fingerprints on the 2002 coup, but Trump broke new ground by admitting he ordered lethal operations. Florida hardliners praised the move and regional governments denounced it. The decision put the CIA in direct conflict with Venezuelan intelligence services. You could see the risk of lethal exchanges spiraling into a wider war.

The September 2025 lethal strikes on Caribbean vessels showed the escalation. U.S. Navy ships, including amphibious assault vessels carrying 2,200 Marines, conducted three separate strikes on boats suspected of drug trafficking from Venezuela. Venezuelan sources reported the attacks killed 37 people, and some reports cited up to 64 civilian deaths. The administration claimed the military conducted law enforcement actions against narco-terrorists, even though the targets floated in international waters. The strikes violated the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, which permits force against civilian vessels under direct threat. The administration notified Congress that the military engaged in a “non-international armed conflict” with designated terrorist organizations. Lawyers invented this legal fiction to bypass the War Powers Resolution. A foreign power launching similar strikes against U.S. vessels would commit an act of war. The United States calls it counter-narcotics.

The military built up forces in the open, deploying the Gerald R. Ford Carrier Strike Group, B-52 and B-1 bombers, and a fleet of eight surface warships plus a nuclear-powered submarine. The Navy positioned 8 percent of the entire U.S. global warship fleet in the Caribbean. The concentration of force dwarfed any counter-narcotics mission. B-52 bombers flew along the Venezuelan coast in a “show of force,” while Army Special Operations aviation conducted exercises in the southern Caribbean. It looked like an invasion force waiting for a pretext. The administration mirrored the 1983 Grenada invasion by planning to create a crisis invoking American vulnerability, then employ overwhelming force. Venezuela is 30 times larger than Grenada and holds 10 times the population. The scale of the buildup made no strategic sense.

Bureaucratic politics drove the escalation. Previous strategies failed, so officials needed to prove “toughness” to domestic constituencies. Secretary of State Marco Rubio advocated regime change for years and pushed for military options over Special Envoy Richard Grenell’s objections. Rubio argued Maduro’s survival after 25 years of U.S. pressure made America look weak, claiming direct action could restore credibility. The argument conflated hegemonic pride with national interest. Maduro’s survival demonstrated the limits of U.S. power. But defiance violates the zero-sum logic of American empire, so the United States cannot allow a small country to resist and stand, because the resistance might inspire others. The logic of this approach leads to war.

Latin American governments despise the 2025 escalation. Every leader pushed back. Mexico’s President López Obrador called the CIA authorization a violation of sovereignty and offered to mediate direct talks. Colombia’s President Gustavo Petro knows the cost of American intervention. He fought as a guerrilla. Petro warned that military action would destabilize the entire Andean region and pledged to keep Colombian territory clear of any invasion.
