### **Introduction**

The US runs over 5,400 data centers, more than any other country, and they consume 45% of the world’s electricity for this one purpose. You mostly don’t notice them from the highway. They run the AI models and the cloud storage behind everything you check on your phone at odd hours. A single hyperscale facility pulls enough power for 80,000 to 100,000 homes and enough water for a small town. People living beside these things in Georgia, Tennessee, Virginia, Arizona have reported contaminated wells and respiratory problems. The cooling systems hum through the night. Utility bills climb past what the household budget can absorb. The tech companies collect billions in tax incentives to build them. The neighbors eat the environmental and financial cost, and the question of who decides where these facilities go, who answers to the residents next door, has grown harder to sidestep as the construction picks up.

### **The Scale of the Surge**

Two new data centers go up in the United States every week. That’s the pace as of March 2025, and it’s turned stretches of Virginia farmland and Texas suburbia into something closer to a refinery district than a neighborhood. The country now runs more than 5,400 of these facilities, up from about 1,400 in 2010, and the buildout clusters hard in a few specific corridors. Drive through Loudoun County, Virginia, and you’ll see why people in the industry call the place Data Center Alley. About 300 data centers sit in Northern Virginia, and the state routes roughly two-thirds of the world’s internet traffic through its servers. A third of the planet’s data packets pass through Loudoun County alone, and Virginia dedicates 26% of its electricity generation to keeping those machines cold and running.

Operators pick sites based on two things they can’t fake: cheap, reliable power and open land. Water access and tax breaks help close the deal, and the companies building these facilities weigh all four factors. Northern Virginia, Dallas-Fort Worth, Phoenix, and Atlanta absorb most of the new construction. Companies are also scouting Columbus, Ohio, and parts of Wyoming, places where zoning fights and grid constraints haven’t caught up to the demand yet. Georgia is growing faster than any other state, and the math explains the appeal. Industrial electricity there runs 42% below the national average, and the state hands out over $180 million in annual tax breaks to data center operators. Development has spread across the Atlanta metro and pushed into rural counties like Fayette and Monroe, where the land is flat and the planning boards are seeing this kind of project for the first time.

AI is the reason the building boom keeps accelerating. Cloud storage and streaming video already filled a lot of square footage. Training and running large language models draws power at a different order of magnitude. AI-specific leasing made up close to 40% of new U.S. data center activity in 2023, and companies bolting language models onto their products will push that share higher. A single Google search with AI features pulls 30 times the electricity of a regular search. Training something like GPT-4 means running thousands of specialized processors around the clock for weeks on end. Meta is building a facility called Hyperion in Louisiana that will eventually cover an area approaching the size of Manhattan. On a peak day it’ll draw more electricity than the entire city of New Orleans uses, and the utility companies are constructing dedicated transmission lines and substations to handle loads measured in gigawatts.

The money involved has no real precedent in industrial real estate. OpenAI and SoftBank’s Project Stargate has committed up to $500 billion for AI infrastructure over the next several years. Meta plans to spend at least $64 billion on facilities and equipment in 2025. Google has earmarked $75 billion. Microsoft, $80 billion. A lot of that cash goes toward buying land, building power infrastructure, and constructing facilities that top a million square feet and pull over 2 terawatt-hours of electricity a year, enough to supply 200,000 homes. And the spending pulls in more spending. Once a company builds the transmission lines and substations for one data center, the next operator looks at the adjacent parcel and sees a site that’s already half-wired. Developers keep stacking on top of each other, and the local impacts compound in ways that are hard to unwind once the concrete is poured and the leases are signed.

### **Power Grids Under Strain**

U.S. data centers pulled 76 terawatt-hours of electricity off the grid in 2018. Five years later, that figure had climbed to 176 terawatt-hours, 4.4% of everything the country consumes. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory projects the number will land somewhere between 325 and 580 terawatt-hours by 2028, which would put data centers at 6.7% to 12% of national electricity. Boston Consulting Group ran its own model and came back with data centers consuming 25% of 2023 generation levels by 2030. Goldman Sachs pegged the share at 8% of national energy usage by the same year. The projections disagree on the exact number, but they all point the same direction. You’re looking at one of the biggest electricity draws in the country within a handful of years, and the utilities that built their demand forecasts around a different era are scrambling to redo the math.

The demand piles up in specific places, and the engineers running those local grids are stretched past what the infrastructure can handle. A standard hyperscale facility pulls 100 megawatts. The newer AI training campuses need 500 megawatts or more. Meta’s Louisiana development will require multiple gigawatts at full buildout, which means the power delivery approach has to be built from scratch. Virginia gives you the clearest picture of what’s already happening. Data centers consumed 26% of the state’s electricity in 2023, and Dominion Energy responded by keeping coal plants running that the state had scheduled to retire and by pushing back the decommissioning of natural gas peaker plants so the lights stay on during peak demand. Dominion has said publicly that it needs to roughly double electricity generation by 2039. The price tag runs up to $103 billion, and residential bills could climb 50% to cover infrastructure that mostly serves data center customers.

The strain goes past generation. Each new facility needs substations and high-voltage transmission lines, and those run hundreds of millions of dollars apiece. In the seven mid-Atlantic states, utilities assigned $4.3 billion in data center connection costs to residential customers in 2024 through rate base accounting. Homeowners are subsidizing industrial expansion. Wholesale electricity costs in data-heavy regions have jumped as much as 267% because utilities have to buy pricier peaking power and capacity reserves to keep up. Georgia’s Public Service Commission approved a 24% residential rate increase on May 1st, the sixth hike since 2023, totaling over 40%. The commission tied the increases to serving new data center load.

Amazon and Microsoft and Google have all issued statements saying they’ll cover the infrastructure costs for their facilities. Dominion Energy’s own projections include residential rate increases to fund expansions that mostly serve data centers. These same companies collect tax exemptions that shrink their contributions to local government services. You end up paying twice. Your electricity bill goes up to build the infrastructure, and your public services get thinner because the tax revenue that would have funded them went to a break for a data campus.

### **Water Wars in the Desert**

Data centers gulp water to keep servers from cooking themselves. The EPA’s latest numbers show direct on-site withdrawals tripled between 2014 and 2023, hitting 66 billion liters (17.4 billion gallons) a year. AI workloads run hotter than the older server farms did, and the industry is shifting toward liquid cooling systems that drink far more than air-based setups ever did. Projections put that 66 billion figure at two to four times its current size by 2028. A single hyperscale facility pulls 3 to 5 million gallons a day. The biggest ones push toward 10 million. In Georgia, a proposed data center would sip up to 6 million gallons daily, which could double the host county’s total water consumption and put the facility in direct competition with the households and farms already drawing from the same supply.

Roughly 43% of large data centers sit in areas the World Resources Institute’s Aqueduct tool classifies as high or extremely high water stress. More than half of Microsoft’s facilities and close to half of Amazon’s are parked in water-scarce regions. The companies picked those spots for cheap land and tax breaks, and the dry climate cuts their cooling energy bills. Arizona, Nevada, and New Mexico have absorbed a wave of new construction. Microsoft alone operates five data centers in Arizona with a combined planned draw of 1.83 billion gallons per year. That’s the annual household need of 61,000 Americans, or a city the size of Santa Cruz, California, pulling from a desert aquifer system where residential users already ration outdoor watering.

Agriculture still accounts for 70 to 80% of water use across Western states, and municipal supplies are tightening under population growth and a warming climate. Colorado River flow has shrunk 20% since 2000 from drought and higher temperatures, and the basin states keep approving new data centers that need guaranteed water allocations. Arizona’s Department of Water Resources manages allocations tightly within designated Active Management Areas, but smaller municipalities outside those zones let companies sink their own wells with no state oversight. Public records requests pulled internal planning documents showing Microsoft budgeted 1 million gallons per day for each Arizona building, drawn from groundwater in a region where farmers and cities have pumped the aquifers past recharge capacity for generations.

Microsoft, Google, and the other hyperscalers have pledged to go “water positive” by 2030, restoring more water than they withdraw through conservation projects and watershed work. The mechanism is mostly credits and offsets. You pay someone downstream to fix a leaky irrigation system or clean up a polluted stream, and you count that against your own withdrawal, the same logic as carbon offset markets. The Trinity Aquifer under Midlothian, Texas, doesn’t care about the accounting. A Google data center there pulled 160 million gallons in 2023 from an aquifer that was already stressed before the facility broke ground. And in Aurora, Colorado, a QTS data center burns 1.1 million gallons of treated municipal water a year irrigating its lawn. The building’s actual cooling loop uses about half that. The grass gets the bigger share.

The xAI facility in Memphis makes the resource question harder to wave away. The company installed 35 gas turbine generators rated at 420 megawatts combined, enough to power 300,000 homes, to back up grid electricity for its servers. The Southern Environmental Law Center ran thermal imaging on the site and found 33 of those 35 turbines running, despite xAI’s public statements that most were inactive and sitting in storage. The turbines vent nitrogen oxides and formaldehyde alongside particulate matter, with no pollution controls attached. Memphis residents breathing that air don’t get a water credit or an offset. They get the exhaust.

### **Air Pollution and Public Health**

Data centers keep multiplying next to neighborhoods, and the diesel generators strapped to them as backup power are doing measurable damage to the people breathing the exhaust. These generators get tested monthly and fire up during utility demand response events, and when they run, they push out nitrogen oxides and fine particulate matter at 200 to 600 times the rate of a modern natural gas plant fitted with selective catalytic reduction. You inhale that cocktail and your lungs and heart pay for it. Asthma attacks spike. Cardiovascular disease worsens. Kids, older adults, and anyone managing a pre-existing condition take the worst of it, and they die younger than they would have a few miles downwind of cleaner air.

A Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health analysis projects that U.S. data center air pollution could kill up to 1,300 people a year by 2030. The public health tab for that, once you tally hospitalizations and lost productivity and the lives cut short, runs close to $20 billion annually. Zoom in on Northern Virginia, where hundreds of data centers crowd the Dulles corridor, and the generator emissions alone carried an estimated $150 million to $300 million health burden in 2023, per EPA benefit-cost models for mortality and morbidity. Then look at Bessemer, Alabama, where industrial zoning dumped polluting infrastructure, data centers included, into rural Black communities that had little political leverage to refuse it. Community health impact assessments pegged the public health damages from data center-related air pollution there at $6 billion in 2023.

The xAI Memphis facility shows you what happens when a company sidesteps regulatory oversight and parks 35 gas turbines in a city that can’t absorb the output. Those turbines can release between 1,000 and 2,000 tons of nitrogen oxides a year, which makes xAI the largest polluter in the Memphis metro area even at half capacity. Memphis leads Tennessee in emergency department visits for asthma and respiratory distress, and the city carries one of the highest rates of toxic air releases in the country because of its industrial history. Easter Knox lives a mile and change from the xAI site. She developed COPD. She never smoked. No one in her family has a history of respiratory disease. Her husband got the same diagnosis, and now they both need daily medication and nebulizer treatments to get through a day. The gas odor and the visible smog wake their neighbors at night, and the stress of breathing that air around the clock has worn people down in ways that show up as anxiety and sleep loss.

In Georgia, construction at a Meta data center sent sediment runoff into local wells and disturbed the groundwater enough to fill residential pipes with clay and sand. One family watched their water run brown from every tap. Lab tests showed total dissolved solids, iron, and manganese levels above EPA secondary drinking water standards. Over two years, that family replaced two washing machines, a dishwasher, a hot water heater. Appliance lifespans dropped by 70% compared to what you’d expect under normal water conditions. The well replacement cost them $20,000. The facility sits 400 yards from their front door, and the light pollution from the campus is so intense that they don’t need nightlights indoors. They navigate their own house by the orange glow bleeding through the windows.

### **Noise and Daily Life Disruption**

Data centers run their cooling systems and fans around the clock, and the sound they produce sits in the 20-200 Hz range, low enough that standard sound meters miss most of it. You can’t hear it the way you hear traffic. You feel it in your sternum, in the glass of water on your nightstand, in the floor joists at 2 a.m. Most local noise ordinances were written for barking dogs and leaf blowers, so they ignore this frequency band entirely. And the health effects kick in at levels well below the decibel thresholds those ordinances even bother to set.

Donna Gallant lives in Prince William County, Virginia, a little over 1,000 feet from a Google data center property line. The tonal noise from the facility gives her anxiety so bad she can’t fall asleep, so she spent $20,000 on extra insulation and specialized sound-dampening windows. The hum still gets through. At night, with the ambient sound gone, it gets worse, and she’s taken to sleeping in industrial noise-canceling headphones. She’s thought about moving her bed to the basement. Her neighbors deal with the same thing, and a few families listed their homes after only a couple of years, though finding a buyer is hard once someone steps onto the porch and hears it.

Carlos Janis, in Manassas, Virginia, walks out to his deck twice a day with a sound meter and logs the numbers. His dataset shows nighttime averages of 45-50 decibels, with low-frequency peaks hitting 65. The World Health Organization recommends 35 decibels for sleeping areas. He’s gathered enough readings to confirm that Amazon’s centers stay within legal limits on paper, but the vibrations rattle his windows under certain atmospheric conditions, and his seven-year-old son started having nightmares a few months back. The kid told his dad there was a spaceship outside the window trying to beam him up. The family moved downstairs for a while. They replaced every upstairs window with triple-pane glass and set up industrial white noise machines, and the hum is still there.

Low-frequency sound doesn’t attenuate the way higher frequencies do. It bends around barriers and sets up structural resonance in walls and floors. Residents compare it to a freight train idling in the yard or a helicopter that circles but never lands. The American Public Health Association links chronic noise exposure to cardiovascular disease through elevated stress hormones and raised blood pressure, and the sleep disruption it causes degrades immune function and mental health over time. You lose focus at work. Your kid can’t concentrate in school.

Jean and Joe Marschall own eight acres in Fayette County, Georgia, and QTS data center construction now surrounds them on three sides. The county development authority approved a 412-acre expansion after packed public hearings where every single resident comment opposed the project. Concrete pours run until 4 a.m. The lights bleed through the bedroom windows. Trucks, cranes, and pile drivers clock 85 decibels at the property line, and the Marschalls have no authority over any of it. The development authority head points to property tax revenue climbing from $36,000 in 2021 to $1.13 million in 2024, with $760,000 of that going to the school board. That math works if you’re averaging across 120,000 county residents. It works less well if you’re one of the 200 people trying to sleep through a concrete pour at 3 in the morning.

### **The Tax Incentive Trap**

Thirty-seven states are handing out tax breaks to lure data centers, and the numbers have gotten absurd. Virginia’s sales tax exemption for data centers cost the state an estimated $928 million in fiscal year 2024. Georgia projects it’ll forgo over $327 million by 2026. Mississippi asks for a $50 million investment and 50 jobs paying 150% of the state average wage before you qualify for property and sales tax breaks, and other states set the bar at $25 million and 25 jobs. You can see the logic each legislature followed: offer the break, get the building, collect the political credit. The math on what comes back, though, doesn’t support the pitch.

The pitch is jobs. A typical hyperscale facility employs 20 to 200 permanent technical staff, and those positions go to specialists recruited nationally, not to the plumber or electrician living three miles from the site. Good Jobs First ran the numbers for several jurisdictions and found the cost can exceed $1 million per job once you account for tax revenue given up over a 15-year abatement window. The Sidecat case in New Albany, Ohio, shows you the whole apparatus at work. An LLC called Sidecat negotiated 100% property tax abatements for 15 years and sales tax exemptions on $2 billion in equipment to put two data centers on 300 acres. The company pocketed at least $60 million in tax savings before anyone in the community figured out Sidecat was Meta, operating through a shell corporation so nobody would recognize the name on the approval paperwork.

The money that doesn’t come in has to come from somewhere, and in practice it comes from the services a township or school district was already stretching to fund. Hilliard, Ohio, lost roughly $2 million from its emergency services budget after 2015 because of property tax abatements tied to data center development. The fire department delayed equipment purchases. It left positions open. Call volumes climbed 300% from data center alarms and emergencies during the same stretch. Michigan went further. Public Act 207 of 2024 exempts qualifying data centers from construction and operational taxes until 2050 or 2065, which covers the useful life of the building and strips property-valuation-based school funding for a generation. Caledonia Community Schools sued over a proposed center’s exemptions that would have cut $4 million a year from the district’s budget, the kind of shortfall that means teacher layoffs and program cuts within a couple of semesters.

Amazon’s facilities in three Northern Virginia counties did contribute $460 million in taxes in 2023, and that’s a real number, though it bundles in taxes paid by employees and secondary businesses alongside the property and equipment levies. You have to weigh it against the infrastructure those counties had to build, the development that never happened on that land, and the residents living next to the humming. In Fayette County, the development authority points to $760,000 contributed to schools as justification for the impact on 200 nearby residents. Those residents measure the cost in contaminated water that means a $20,000 well replacement, in sleepless nights that end in a doctor’s office, in home equity that real estate studies peg at 30 to 40% below what the house would fetch without the facility next door. The $760,000 doesn’t buy any of that back.

### **The Accountability Gap**

Amazon reports a global Power Usage Effectiveness of 1.15, ahead of the 1.25 industry average. Google runs a fleet-wide PUE of 1.09. Microsoft hits around 1.12 to 1.15 globally through advanced cooling designs. These are strong numbers, and the companies have matched 100% of their electricity consumption with renewable energy purchases through power purchase agreements and renewable energy certificates. Several have pledged 24/7 carbon-free energy by 2030, meaning every hour of consumption gets matched with carbon-free generation. The sustainability narratives built on these metrics point your attention toward the global ledger and away from what’s happening at the county level.

Through their accounting, the companies separate the brand-level claim from the resource reality on the ground. A data center powered by 100% renewables through RECs can still drive water scarcity in a drought-stricken county, because the certificates don’t require clean power to reach the specific grid where the facility operates. Backup generators running during outages still produce NOx emissions linked to respiratory illness in nearby neighborhoods, and property tax abatements that the company negotiates separately from its sustainability metrics still cut into school budgets. The net-zero goal applies to the company’s global ledger and brand identity. The carrying capacity of the local watershed and the municipal budget’s ability to fund services sit outside that ledger.

Companies pull in billions in tax incentives and buy renewable energy at industrial rate schedules that run below what residential customers pay. Local governments and residents absorb the costs: depleted water tables, NOx-heavy air, a tax base eroded enough to show up as deferred road repairs and underfunded classrooms. The people dealing with those consequences have little negotiating power against trillion-dollar corporations. Legal and financial structures insulate the companies through limited liability corporations, non-disclosure agreements, and contractual provisions that cap their infrastructure payment obligations.

In Georgia, residents near the Meta data center found through campaign finance disclosures that state legislators who blocked Senate Bill 34 received significant campaign contributions from Georgia Power and its executives. The bill would have required data centers to pay their own operational costs rather than shifting them to ratepayers. It died on Crossover Day without a committee vote, despite bipartisan support from consumer advocates. Patty Durand, a former Georgia Public Service Commissioner who now runs the consumer advocacy group Energy Rights, calls the arrangement clearly corrupting. She points out that the poorest region in the country pays the most to utility CEOs earning $23 to $33 million annually, while residential rates have climbed six times in two years.

### **The Net-Zero Illusion**

Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Meta. You’ve seen the press releases. “100% renewable energy!” with a nice leaf graphic and a link to a 90-page PDF nobody reads. The fine print tells a different story, and the fine print is where the whole claim lives. These companies match their electricity consumption with renewable energy certificates, or RECs, which are paper instruments representing the environmental attributes of clean generation somewhere else. They don’t require a single electron of that power to reach the data center’s actual location. So a facility in Virginia, sitting on a grid that’s 60% natural gas and nuclear, buys wind RECs from a Texas ranch and calls itself 100% renewable. The servers keep humming along on fossil gas. The certificate says otherwise.

The newer pledge is 24/7 carbon-free energy by 2030, where every hour of consumption gets matched with carbon-free generation on the same regional grid. You can see why this is harder. It demands new storage, new transmission lines, new generation capacity built in the right places at the right times. Google and Microsoft have both signed on. But even if they pull it off, the metric stays locked on carbon. Water consumption, land use, the communities living next to these facilities, none of that enters the spreadsheet. And the full lifecycle picture is uglier than any operational metric captures: embodied carbon baked into the concrete and steel at construction, electronic waste from servers swapped out every three to five years, the heat-island effect of a million-square-foot building with a dark roof absorbing solar radiation all summer and baking the neighborhood around it.

Water gets the same accounting treatment. The pledge is “water positive by 2030,” achieved through restoration projects and conservation investments in some other part of the watershed. A data center in Arizona pumps from a depleted aquifer, and the company funds an agricultural efficiency project two states over, and the ledger balances. The local aquifer still drops. The springs dry up. The community’s wells go deeper every year. Google’s data center in Midlothian, Texas pulled 160 million gallons from the Trinity Aquifer in 2023, an aquifer that was already stressed before the facility broke ground. The water-positivity claim rests on projects restoring irrigation efficiency hundreds of miles away. The people in Midlothian don’t drink irrigation efficiency.

Microsoft is piloting cross-laminated timber construction that cuts embodied carbon by up to 65% compared to concrete, and AWS used lower-carbon materials across 43 new facilities in 2023, saving over 22,000 tonnes of CO2 equivalent. Those are real gains in materials science and procurement, and the engineers doing that work deserve credit. But each new million-square-foot facility still represents tens of thousands of tons of material pulled out of the ground, processed, shipped, and poured. Meta is building a data center roughly the size of Manhattan. Cut the embodied carbon per square foot in half and you’ve still got a footprint that dwarfs the savings. The incremental improvement gets swallowed by the sheer volume of new construction.

You can make the efficiency numbers look good forever. PUE drops, WUE drops, the sustainability report gets another green checkmark. And then the company builds forty more data centers to handle the next wave of AI training runs, and absolute consumption climbs past whatever the efficiency gains bought you. The net-zero framing lets you keep growing the physical infrastructure exponentially while calling the whole operation sustainable, as long as the per-unit metrics trend in the right direction. The math breaks down for the community watching its groundwater table fall or breathing the diesel exhaust from backup generators during a grid failure. And underneath all the accounting, there’s a question these reports never ask: whether every AI application and digital service being built right now is worth the resource cost of building and running it. The efficiency dashboard answers a different question. It answers whether you’re wasting less per unit. It doesn’t answer whether you should be building the unit at all.

### **Regulatory Failures and Federal Preemption**

Congress and the agencies it oversees have spent the last three years watching data centers multiply across the country while the rules meant to govern them stayed frozen in a pre-AI world. You end up in a situation where a company’s own press release about “sustainability goals” carries more regulatory weight than any enforceable public standard. The federal government made that gap wider on purpose. Executive Order 14318, signed in July 2025, tells agencies to fast-track permitting for large-scale AI data centers by expanding categorical exclusions under the National Environmental Policy Act and rewriting Clean Air and Clean Water Act regulations so that review timelines collapse from years down to months.

Trump declared a national energy emergency in January 2025 with the stated goal of cutting permitting red tape for data centers and AI facilities. He then moved the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, which had operated as an independent and nonpartisan body overseeing interstate electricity transmission, under direct White House control through executive action. Trump said out loud, at a press event, that he could approve projects himself under emergency declaration authority and skip years of environmental review and public comment. The administration packaged all of this as a national security imperative. You delay a data center, the argument goes, and you hand China an edge in AI development. Any requirement for an environmental study becomes, in that framing, a threat to the country.

States pushed back, and the pushback got specific. Oregon’s POWER Act (HB 3546) forces facilities over 20 megawatts into 10-year contracts that make them pay their share of grid expansion costs, so utilities can’t dump those expenses onto residential ratepayers. Texas SB-6 and New Jersey passed similar requirements tying large data centers to interconnection and transmission upgrade costs. California’s AB 222 would make AI model operators report energy usage, water consumption, and the proportion drawn from California’s grid to the state Energy Commission, which sets a precedent for targeted oversight that no other state has attempted at that granularity. Minnesota requires a separate water-use permit process for data centers. Indiana introduced legislation demanding local site assessments that evaluate impacts on water and natural resources before any approval goes through.

But the tools for enforcement stay blunt. Tech companies lean on nondisclosure agreements to keep consumption data and operational parameters like generator testing schedules out of public view, which makes it hard for a community to argue its case when it can’t see the numbers. In Virginia, Governor Glenn Youngkin vetoed a 2025 bill that would have required rezoning applicants to assess sound and water impacts, a move that showed how much political resistance still exists in Republican-led states that treat business development as the only metric that counts. You’re left with a group of communities absorbing the costs of rapid expansion while the industry’s economic and strategic weight makes most legislators reluctant to slow anything down. The people paying higher electric bills and watching their water tables drop are the ones stuck asking for protections that the regulatory structure, as it currently stands, was never built to deliver.

### **Future Trajectories and Community Responses**

Meta plans to pour $64 billion into facilities in 2025. Google committed $75 billion. Microsoft set aside $80 billion. Amazon expects to spend over $50 billion in capital expenditures, with a big chunk going to data center infrastructure. The Department of Energy projects data centers could pull 12% of total U.S. electricity by 2028, as companies fold AI into medical diagnostics and financial services, education and government operations. Goldman Sachs puts the number at 8% of national energy usage by 2030. That’s the equivalent of 15 to 20 new large nuclear plants or hundreds of utility-scale solar farms.

Nebraska’s two largest public utilities postponed planned coal plant closures to meet data center demand, extending operations into the 2040s. The North Omaha coal station released CO2 at an estimated 300,000 kg per hour in June 2023, according to satellite monitoring data. Two new natural gas plants are in the works for the state, both to serve data centers and AI training facilities. Utilities in Wisconsin, Indiana, and Missouri are doing the same thing, delaying coal retirements and proposing new fossil fuel infrastructure to handle what they call “unprecedented load growth.” Eighty to ninety percent of that growth traces back to data centers.

Microsoft signed a deal to buy all the power from Three Mile Island’s nuclear plant when it reopens in 2027. That’s 835 megawatts of carbon-free baseload power. Amazon put $500 million into X-energy to develop small modular reactors that could sit right at data center campuses. Google and Microsoft are testing advanced geothermal and long-duration battery storage. They’re also working on green hydrogen. All of it aims at their 24/7 carbon-free targets. Scaled up, these investments could change how the grid works. Right now, they cover a small fraction of the power that projected data center growth will demand.

In Fayette County, Georgia, residents formed a nonprofit called Protect Fayette County. They’ve filed lawsuits and petitioned for ordinances requiring minimum setbacks from homes. They also lobbied for a moratorium on new data center approvals until the county adopts comprehensive regulations. In Memphis, the Memphis Community Against Pollution coalition retained environmental attorneys and filed Title VI civil rights complaints with EPA, arguing discriminatory siting in Black communities. Monthly protests at the xAI facility have pulled national media coverage to the permitting failures. In Prince William County, Virginia, a bipartisan group of supervisors proposed a data center “opportunity zone” to concentrate development away from residential areas. The compromise acknowledges the economic value while protecting neighborhoods, though nobody knows yet whether it’ll get implemented.

The next decade of data center expansion will shape American communities and their energy systems. It’ll determine the environmental quality residents can expect. Getting to a sustainable and equitable outcome means governments setting enforceable standards and requiring companies to internalize the environmental and social costs they’ve been externalizing. Community benefit agreements and true cost accounting need to be part of any framework that lasts. The window for democratic institutions to set those terms is narrowing. Resource constraints and community backlash will eventually make the current pace of expansion impossible to sustain, regardless of what any boardroom decides.

### **Conclusion**

Americans living next to data centers are paying for someone else’s server rack. The facilities process the transactions and stream the video that the rest of us scroll through without a second thought, and the companies running them collect billions in public tax breaks while hiring a skeleton crew to keep the lights on. Meanwhile the neighbors eat the noise and the diesel exhaust. Their aquifer drops. And their utility bill climbs because the local grid now serves a 50-megawatt load before it serves the subdivision two miles down the road. You can fix most of this with four policy moves, and none of them require new technology. Make operators pay the full cost of the water and power they pull. Tie every tax incentive to a jobs number the company has to hit or repay. Require environmental review that a resident can read and challenge in plain language. Void the nondisclosure agreements that let a county commission sign away a town’s groundwater without the town finding out for eighteen months. Skip those steps and the pattern holds. The company books its quarterly earnings, the rezoning hearing draws eleven people, and the family next door keeps running the tap and hoping the color clears. They don’t get a press conference. They get a water bill and a headache and a property value that won’t recover, while the state legislature debates whether the next incentive package should be $400 million or $600 million. The companies need the public’s tolerance to keep building. They’re spending it faster than they’re earning it back.
