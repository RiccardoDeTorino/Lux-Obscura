Introduction
Corporate executives bought the United States federal government. And they write the laws. Executives at Apple, Google, Meta, NVIDIA, Intel, Amazon, Microsoft, Tesla, and BlackRock treat lobbying and campaign donations as standard business expenses while they swap personnel with federal agencies to kill bad rules and grab taxpayer money. Corporate lawyers draft the legislation and the voters sit at home to read the final text on their phones. But read the federal lobbying disclosures yourself. You’ll find the tech giants pick the agency staff when you sit down and review the mountains of paperwork they submit to the ethics office. Agency directors run their departments as subsidiaries for the biggest companies on earth. The shareholders win.

Lobbying as Legislative Preemption
Big tech and finance firms write the laws and the industry dropped the old defensive posture. They went on the attack. Seven tech companies spent $61.5 million on federal lobbying in 2024. That figure exceeds their 2020 total by 46 percent. The group includes Meta and Alphabet. ByteDance and Microsoft join OpenAI, Snap and X on the roster. OpenSecrets tracked those 2025 filings and found that Meta handed over $24.4 million in 2024. That sum represents a high watermark for any internet outfit. The company pushed another $13.8 million through the doors in the first six months of 2025, and Axios reported those numbers. You don’t see scattered cash from these firms. They aim their funds at precise statutes and specific agency rules.

The Kids Online Safety Act shows this offensive strategy in action. Lawmakers introduced the bill in 2022 and the Senate passed the measure in April 2024 with a 91 to 3 vote. The legislation threatened platforms with design-level liability for minor harms, and lawmakers placed algorithmic amplification of bad material under that umbrella. Meta assembled a lobbying team to kill the House vote. OpenSecrets counted 59 total lobbyists on the Meta payroll in the first months of 2024. Forty-three of those hires brought former government badges to the job. The company paid for op-eds in regional newspapers and bought full-page ads in the Washington Post to warn readers about free speech overreach. Meta sent NetChoice to buttonhole Republican members of the House Energy and Commerce Committee. Issue One tracked that trade association deployment. The committee killed the bill in June 2024 and lawmakers skipped the floor vote.

Meta repeated this playbook at the state level. The company handed $327,000 to local media outlets in Minnesota during the first months of 2024. Meta purchased loud opposition with those checks to fight the state Age-Appropriate Design Code Act. And Google fought identical legislation. Politico reported that Google bumped state lobbying expenditures from $480,000 in 2021 to $790,000 in 2023. Executives achieved two goals with this multi-jurisdictional pressure campaign. Lobbyists stopped stronger state laws with that cash, and they used a patchwork excuse to demand federal preemption.

Lobbyists built their next campaign on that excuse. Trade group Incompas pushed a provision into the FY2025 omnibus appropriations bill during the final months of 2024. Former Congressman Rick Boucher runs Incompas. His team wrote a ten-year moratorium on state and local AI regulation into the draft. Meta and Amazon funded the push. The Business & Human Rights Resource Centre documented their financial backing. But public anger forced lawmakers to strip the text from the final bill. Senator Ted Cruz didn’t abandon the discarded language. He announced plans in August 2025 to sponsor the moratorium as standalone legislation. TechPolicy.Press reported on the revival. The senator kept the original strategy alive.

NVIDIA executives placed corporate priorities above public interest concerns. OpenSecrets tracked their lobbying spend. The company paid lobbyists $1.6 million during the first half of 2025. Executives increased their lobbying budget by 388 percent from the previous year. NVIDIA hired those lobbyists to pressure the Commerce Department and Congress regarding semiconductor export controls. And the executives wanted to loosen restrictions on H20 chip sales to China. Engineers designed the H20 model to comply with U.S. export rules. They built the chip with enough processing power to run advanced AI training. Biden administration officials granted NVIDIA a license to resume H20 shipments in April 2025. NVIDIA secured the license by agreeing to share 15 percent of its China revenue with the U.S. Treasury. CSET reported on the agreement. Officials in the Trump administration formalized the arrangement. Regulators converted regulatory relief into public revenue. You see the government acting as a profit-sharing partner.

Intel lobbyists write industrial policy inside corporate boardrooms. OpenSecrets tracks the company spending $6.34 million on federal lobbying in 2024. The lobbying team devoted the majority of those funds to CHIPS and Science Act implementation. They filed 47 LD-2 disclosure forms during that same year and targeted appropriations subcommittees with those documents. And Intel representatives argued for relaxed labor and environmental standards for semiconductor fabrication plants. Committee members inserted new language into the FY2025 Defense bill in July 2024. Lawmakers waived prevailing wage requirements for CHIPS-funded projects in national security corridors. Lawmakers omitted that term from prior legislation. Intel submitted a white paper in March 2024 containing the exact phrase. Congress published the bill text in the Congressional Record on July 11, 2024.

BlackRock executives handed over $2.88 million to lobbyists in 2024. Dalia Blass runs the government affairs desk and she spends that cash chasing systemic regulatory frameworks because those broad rules fit the asset management model. She wants the whole board tilted in her favor. Dalia Blass, is a former SEC Director of the Division of Investment Management. And she knows the agency. Her team filed a 42-page comment letter under SEC Comment File No. S7-10-22-042123 to attack mandatory Scope 3 emissions reporting. Blass argued the paperwork created operational burdens disproportionate to investor utility. The SEC caved. The agency dropped the Scope 3 requirement in March 2025. You can find the official retreat buried in SEC Press Release 2025-51.

Campaign Finance as Relationship Infrastructure
Corporate executives use campaign money to buy access and secure political alignment. Congress banned direct corporate donations to candidates so companies bypass this restriction using political action committees and bundled employee checks. Managers use internal scorecards and policy metrics to guide these bundled donations. The playbook is strict. You won’t find random charity in the paperwork.

Apple executives don’t use a corporate political action committee so the tech giant routed $5.32 million through individual employee wallets during the 2020 to 2024 election cycle. Kamala Harris received $1.99 million. The Democratic National Committee secured $550,399. And OpenSecrets logged these totals in 2025. Apple executives aimed this cash at a specific regulatory target. Harris ran an antitrust probe into the App Store in 2019 during her tenure as California attorney general before she dropped the investigation and skipped enforcement. Apple executives celebrated the outcome in internal risk assessments. The management team called the dropped probe a victory for developer stability in their 2020 10-K filing on page 38.

Meta executives launched a political action committee in 2011 and wrote checks for $423,397 during the 2023 to 2024 cycle while employees donated $5.11 million during that period. Democratic candidates captured 92.35 percent of the candidate money. Meta executives sent $242,372 to the Republican National Committee and OpenSecrets recorded these splits in 2025. Meta executives fund both parties to keep friends in the White House and the Senate. They buy insurance against a divided Congress. And you see the strategy in the donation dates. Kamala Harris locked up the Democratic nomination in December 2024. Meta workers don’t trickle money out the door. They sent $840,000 in one week. You can find this spike in the Federal Election Commission transaction records from December 9 to 15, marking the largest total in a single week in company history.

Intel plays a balanced game. The PAC wrote $30,000 checks to four committees in 2024. The Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee got one. The National Republican Senatorial Committee got another. The House campaign arms for both parties received the same amount, according to a February 2025 FEC filing. Employees tilted their cash toward Democrats at 58 percent. And Republicans took the remaining 42 percent. You don’t see Intel picking sides. You get bipartisan backing for CHIPS Act funding requests either way. The Senate Appropriations Subcommittee on Commerce, Justice, and Science held a closed-door briefing on semiconductor security in February 2025. Intel sent its government relations vice president Steven McGeady. He sat as the lone industry representative in the room, per a February 12 LegiStorm event log.

BlackRock’s PAC pushed out $937,000 between January 2023 and October 2024. Democrats grabbed 65 percent of that money, OpenSecrets data shows. You can track a cleaner story through personnel moves. Brian Deese left his chief strategist post at BlackRock to direct the National Economic Council from 2021 to 2023. He designed the Inflation Reduction Act’s clean energy tax credit structure. That structure allocated $369 billion in subsidies. A lot of that cash went to firms where BlackRock holds a top-10 shareholder spot. Tesla and Microsoft are on that list. NextEra Energy joined them, according to an August 16, 2022 White House fact sheet. Deese walked back through BlackRock’s doors in January 2024. He took the title of Vice Chairman. He ran the climate investing division. A January 8 press release confirmed the move.

The Trump transition exposed the transaction costs. Tech CEOs like Elon Musk and Tim Cook cut $1 million checks to Trump’s inaugural committee in January 2025. Sundar Pichai, Mark Zuckerberg, and Satya Nadella did the same, according to a January 22 letter from Senator Elizabeth Warren. The Department of Justice dropped three pending antitrust investigations within 60 days. Prosecutors killed probes into Apple’s app store practices and Amazon’s use of third-party seller data. They tossed the Google ad tech stack case. An August 14, 2025 Prospect article noted the dismissals. The FTC joined the retreat. The agency withdrew its administrative complaint against Meta over teen data collection on March 11. The FTC had filed that case in November 2024 under Docket No. 9405.

The Revolving Door as Institutional Capture
Corporate lobbyists use the revolving door as their central mechanism. Agency directors avoid hiring independent staff for key roles. They pick staffers tied to corporate paychecks. Public Citizen researchers tracked top FTC officials from 2000 to 2022 and found 75 percent took tech sector jobs after leaving the government. And Antitrust Division prosecutors run the exact same playbook.

Eighteen former Justice Department and FTC antitrust lawyers took Big Tech paychecks between 2014 and 2021. Nine took jobs at Amazon and four went to Meta. Lawyers walk through the door in both directions. You watch the same lawyers draft strict consent decrees on Monday and see them quit on Tuesday to advise those exact corporations on dodging the new rules. Forty-one current Justice Department employees review mergers after working for Amazon and Microsoft.

Meta executives embed their people inside the FTC. Five former Meta policy directors took government jobs between 2020 and 2023, and Joel Kaplan and Jennifer Newstead grabbed the top spots. Kaplan carried serious baggage from his time running the Meta Washington office, where he directed a 2021 lobbying push against the Kids Online Safety Act. FTC directors assigned him to the digital markets unit handling the Meta purchase of Within, a virtual reality fitness app developer. Staff economists warned the deal would entrench Meta dominance in immersive social platforms. FTC commissioners ignored the warnings and approved the purchase in February 2023.

The Defense Department runs the same scheme. Doug Beck left his job as Apple’s vice president for government affairs and took the helm of the Defense Innovation Unit in January 2025. Beck shoved a $450 million contract toward Palantir for an artificial intelligence battlefield logistics system. He skipped the usual competitive bidding process. Beck cited an “urgent operational need” to justify the rush, according to DoD Contract Award W911QX-25-C-0017, dated March 3, 2025. He spent 2021 and 2022 sitting on the DIU Industry Advisory Council representing Apple.

You find the deepest pipeline at elite law firms. The Revolving Door Project analyzed DOJ and FTC departures from 2018 to 2024. They found 61 percent of departing attorneys and economists took jobs at firms like Gibson Dunn and Kirkland & Ellis. And these firms defend major tech clients in antitrust matters. Bruce Hoffman left his post as FTC Bureau of Competition director and joined Jones Day in 2020. His team represented Amazon in the FTC v. Amazon trial in 2023. They argued Amazon’s use of third-party seller data amounted to “standard industry practice.” Internal Amazon documents showed the practice generated $1.2 billion in private-label sales in 2022, according to U.S. v. Amazon, Trial Exhibit 1142.

Regulatory Capture Through Administrative Law
Corporate lobbyists capture regulatory agencies through incremental, technical interventions in rulemaking. You find the real work in the margins of federal registers. Officials at the FTC and SEC run notice-and-comment procedures. Lobbyists dictate final rules. Lawyers use a well-placed footnote in a draft rule to bypass congressional lobbying.

You see the identical blueprint in the 2023 FCC broadband privacy rule. Officials at the agency wanted to restore ISP data opt-in requirements. Lobbyists for Google and Amazon submitted joint comments. Tech executives pushed a technology-neutral standard to cover edge providers and claimed fairness while causing complete gridlock. And commissioners backed down because they feared legal challenges from ISPs and platforms. They restricted the rule to sensitive data categories. Commissioners granted exemptions to browsing history and app usage under FCC 21-48 that they adopted in December 2023. Lobbyists at Google drafted the technology-neutral language in a September 2023 memo and filed that document as Comment No. EA-00128912.

It’s the same story with ESG disclosures. Executives at BlackRock and Vanguard formed the Net Zero Asset Managers initiative in 2022 to pledge net-zero emissions in their portfolios by 2050. Commissioners at the SEC proposed mandatory climate risk disclosures. Lawyers for BlackRock submitted a comment letter urging narrow definitions and argued that portfolio alignment metrics require fund-level aggregates. These lawyers shielded colossal holdings like Exxon and Chevron from scrutiny with this specific position. SEC commissioners adopted this approach in the March 2025 final rule that they released under Release No. 33-11216.

Corporate lawyers dull the teeth of antitrust enforcers. Bureaucrats at the Federal Trade Commission botched the 2007 DoubleClick buyout and staff economists pushed for a second information request to dig deeper into the messy deal. The deputy director killed the idea. A former Microsoft lobbyist held that job. He wrote that combining ad tech platforms benefits competition in a December 2007 memo and government archivists declassified that exact document in 2023. Regulators didn’t catch it. Google used DoubleClick code to build a vast ad revenue stream. The company pulled in a mere $16.1 billion back in 2007 and built a $224.5 billion empire by 2024. You can’t ignore those numbers in the 2024 Alphabet 10-K filing.

Lawmakers cripple their own agencies through the revolving door. Federal Trade Commission staff attorneys encounter post-employment restrictions and agency bosses ban the lawyers from working on matters related to their government work for two years. Agency bosses interpret that phrase to cover entire industry sectors. Top engineers don’t stay. Researchers at Brookings published a study in 2024 and they found a stark education gap. Twelve percent of Federal Trade Commission economists held PhDs in computer science or data science. Google staffed 68 percent of its antitrust economics team with PhDs in those exact fields. Tech giants use this gap to deploy technical arguments about algorithmic bias and data portability while regulators lack the capacity to rebut those claims.

Case Study: The R&D Expensing Windfall
Lobbyists and executives used the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill tax package to secure massive corporate benefits. Lawmakers wrote Section 174 of the tax code in 2022 and forced companies to amortize domestic research expenses over five years while tech executives couldn’t stop complaining about stifled innovation. And corporate leaders at Apple, Amazon, Meta, Alphabet, and Tesla increased research spending between 18% and 43% during those two years.

Industry insiders started the campaign in the first quarter of 2024 when executives at five tech firms including Apple and Google formed the Innovation Investment Coalition. They hired Brownstein Hyatt Farber Schreck and BGR Group to lobby Congress. Coalition members spent $63.2 million during 2024 and 2025. And they demanded full reinstatement of immediate expensing for domestic research retroactive to 2022 alongside catch-up deductions the following year.

Lobbyists secured the legislation with campaign contributions. Coalition political action committee members gave $4.8 million to House Ways and Means Committee members in the fourth quarter of 2024. Committee Chair Jason Smith received $120,000 and Ranking Member Richard Neal took $95,000. Intel lobbyists drafted the technical amendment and created Section 104 of the final bill to let firms claim all unamortized costs in the subsequent tax season.

Lawmakers produced a $75 billion windfall for five firms with the legislation. Alphabet claimed $24 billion. Amazon took $22 billion while Meta secured $15 billion and Apple collected $10 billion. Tesla received $2.5 billion. Accountants calculated the effective federal tax rate for Alphabet and watched it drop from 16.2% to negative 3.1% between 2022 and 2024. Senator Elizabeth Warren noted the $75 billion could fund a full year of SNAP benefits for 12 million Americans.

Twenty-one states passed copycat privacy laws by November 2025 according to the IAPP. Oregon tried a different approach. Drafters put a private right of action in their initial 2023 bill, HB 3130, and TechNet sent their lobbyists to the conference committee. And the Chamber of Commerce joined the battle. Those hired guns forced the sponsors to delete the lawsuit provision before the final vote.

Senators designed the federal American Privacy Rights Act to mirror this exact playbook. Senator Maria Cantwell and Representative Cathy McMorris Rodgers negotiated the current draft and omitted employee data from the protections. The lawmakers allowed first-party marketing and they ignore user consent. They wrote a preemption clause to wipe out stronger state laws. Meta and Google handed over 3.2 million dollars each to lobbyists during the third quarter of 2025 according to OpenSecrets. They bought this specific text.

Legislators built this low regulatory floor to function as a hard ceiling. California passed the strongest state law, the CPRA, and lawmakers let businesses charge fees for data deletion requests. Walmart demands 45 dollars to process a single deletion request. Target takes 38 dollars while Amazon collects 29 dollars to process the exact same request according to a CDT survey. So regular shoppers lack the funds to exercise their legal rights.

Alphabet pocketed $11.9 billion through this deduction between 2018 and 2023. Meta grabbed another $6.34 billion while Apple kept $9.1 billion. The Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy tallied these exact figures in 2024. They don’t hide these numbers. That missing revenue left Washington short on hard cash. That $27.3 billion shortfall exceeds the entire annual budget for the Department of Housing and Urban Development.

Lawmakers reinstated research and development expensing in 2025. Congress applied the provision after the fact to turn old tax bills into fresh refunds and expand the wealth transfer. Apple paid a $19.5 billion federal tax bill in 2022 before accountants logged a $1.2 billion credit in 2025, per Note 12 of the corporate 10-K filing. Tesla executives reported a 9 percent tax rate for 2022. The Tesla finance team recorded a negative 22 percent rate by 2024 on page 89 of their 2025 10-K.

Politicians from both parties approved this wealth transfer when the House passed the research and development provision 298 to 124. Forty-one Democrats voted yes. Thirty-eight of those Democrats took over $50,000 from tech political action committees during the 2024 election cycle. And public records from House Vote No. 512 on July 18, 2025 and the Federal Election Commission show the money trail.

Democratic Erosion Metrics

You read the raw math in the 2010 Hacker and Pierson paper. Hacker updated his figures this year. The wealthy watched their influence score climb to 0.84. Hacker assigned the bottom half a score of -0.11 for the recent update. And you see the divide widen.

Legislators don’t hide this bias in their daily work. The 116th Congress passed 412 public laws between 2019 and 2020. The 118th Congress managed a meager 289 laws during the 2023 and 2024 sessions.
