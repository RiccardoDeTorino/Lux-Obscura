### **Introduction**

*Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton* came down from the Supreme Court on June 27, 2025. The Court swapped strict scrutiny for intermediate scrutiny in age verification cases, and that swap does more than shift legal doctrine. It reorders priorities. State interests in regulating content now outweigh adult privacy and the right to anonymous speech online. Texas House Bill 1181 sits at the center of the case. The bill requires commercial websites carrying substantial amounts of sexual material harmful to minors to verify a user’s age, either through government identification or transactional data analysis. The Court called privacy concerns “incidental burdens.” Adults, the Court said, hold no First Amendment right to dodge age verification. That ruling opens a door for states to expand surveillance into lawful online activity. This exposé tracks Paxton’s fallout across two fronts: the international models other countries have already tried, and the technical flaws built into verification systems themselves. It documents harm landing hardest on vulnerable groups. Lawmakers have also paired these laws with deregulation that strips away accountability. A final section looks at resistance strategies and privacy-preserving alternatives. Those could chart a different course.

### **From Strict Scrutiny to Intermediate Scrutiny**

The Supreme Court split 6-3 in *Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton*. Six justices backed Texas, not nine. Justice Clarence Thomas wrote the majority opinion. Roberts, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett joined him. Kagan dissented. Sotomayor and Jackson signed on with her. The Court broke from decades of First Amendment doctrine protecting online speech. For years, judges applied strict scrutiny to content-based laws. That standard forced the government to prove a compelling interest and pick the least restrictive option on the table. Federal courts leaned on it again and again, striking down age verification mandates across the country. Their logic held that forcing adults to hand over personal information to view lawful content carries constitutional weight. Paxton tears that framework down. Thomas swaps in intermediate scrutiny. Under this test, a law needs only an “important” government interest and a “substantial” connection to that goal.

Clarence Thomas wrote the majority opinion. Roberts signed on. So did Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett. Six votes total, and Thomas uses them to shrink privacy concerns down to almost nothing while handing states new room to regulate. He calls the burden incidental. Hand a government ID to some website, that counts. Let a third-party vendor log your purchase history, that counts too. Thomas treats both as minor costs of reaching adult content, a light tax on speech at most. With that framing, Thomas wipes out a protection courts spent decades building: the right to speak without identifying yourself. Adults have no First Amendment right to skip age verification, the majority says. Read that sentence at face value, and Thomas shuts the door on digital anonymity as a constitutional principle, one lower courts had been building toward for years. He states it in the opinion itself. Social media platforms could be next. Search engines too. Texas kept its law narrow, but the Court treats that narrowness as a policy choice Texas made, one it could widen later if lawmakers choose. Nothing in the reasoning stops at adult content sites.

Elena Kagan wrote the dissent. Sonia Sotomayor and Ketanji Brown Jackson signed her opinion. These three justices accused their colleagues of picking a preferred ruling before examining the legal scrutiny standards and manipulating those rules to justify a predetermined conclusion. The dissenting justices argued the state prevents adults from reading material protected by the Constitution. The court must apply the strictest judicial review to this restriction. And the authors built the whole legal mechanism around privacy compliance. The government compels millions of adults to make a harsh decision, and citizens must surrender personal data to access legal websites. The lawyers and justices are fighting a broader constitutional battle over the First Amendment in digital spaces because they don’t agree on the scope of anonymous internet browsing. One faction believes the amendment protects anonymous digital participation. The opposing faction believes the amendment protects the spoken or written words themselves and permits endless identity verification demands.

Texas lawmakers pushed HB 1181 through the statehouse, and judges stamped the identity checks that legislators wrote into the bill. You upload a digital government ID. Or you let a private vendor scan your mortgage and employment files, and you tie your legal name to your browser history, dragging a permanent surveillance footprint years after you clear the initial age gate. The Court deferred to state politicians to evaluate the mechanics of these systems, and the justices built a brick wall for future plaintiffs fighting the statute in appellate courts. Lower courts apply intermediate scrutiny. Judges applying this intermediate standard uphold regulations that they would void under strict scrutiny.

### **Comparative Regulatory Models: Us, UK, and Australia**

American lawmakers assembled an age verification patchwork, and two dozen state legislatures wrote distinct rules and penalties before 2024 ended. Texas and Louisiana politicians drafted the strictest mandates. Website operators must obey them. Lawmakers force platforms to collect government identification or run transactional data checks on sites hosting restricted material. South Carolina legislators copied this blueprint for their Child Online Safety Act and added state-approved digitized identification alongside third-party database lookups. Congress omitted a central oversight body. State attorneys general hold the enforcement authority while federal judges evaluate the statutory language using the intermediate scrutiny standard Ken Paxton established. Each judge applies a personal interpretation to the text.

Seven percent of American adults live outside the government identification system. Demographers watch that percentage climb among lower-income individuals and young adults who don’t drive. Black and Hispanic communities experience wider documentation gaps. State officials strip residents of lawful online content and create digital disenfranchisement. Texas lawmakers proposed Know Your Customer verification for weather applications. They extended identification requirements into routine digital life and forced a person downloading a rain forecast to pass through a surveillance checkpoint. District judges issued injunctions grounded in pre-Paxton strict scrutiny and created a direct conflict with the new standard. The Supreme Court must resolve these circuit splits, and operators sort through contradictory orders because the justices won’t issue a final decision for months.

The United Kingdom picked a different route. Parliament passed the Online Safety Act 2023 and put Ofcom, the communications regulator, in charge of enforcement. The law demands “highly effective age assurance” for high-risk content, pornography and self-harm material chief among them. Ofcom threw out self-declaration and the tick-box approach in its guidance. Checkboxes are gone. The regulator backs document checks now, photo ID or credit card verification, plus AI-driven facial age estimation. UK GDPR governs the whole system too. Its data minimization and purpose limitation rules apply throughout, and British users end up with privacy protections that go beyond what American state laws provide.

The UK law hit a wall the moment enforcement kicked in. VPN downloads jumped 1,400 percent in July 2025, after porn sites switched on their age checks. Users reached for privacy tools and routed around the barriers. They treat the law as a speed bump on the way to unregulated platforms. Those platforms carry their own data risks. Parliament hasn’t passed dedicated facial recognition legislation. The Information Commissioner’s Office fills that gap with ad-hoc guidance. Regulators work with blind spots, and those spots widen as verification systems spread into new corners of the internet. Ofcom hands companies clearer compliance steps than the US system does. Motivated users find the cracks anyway.

Australian officials split the job between two bodies: the eSafety Commissioner and the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner. Their Social Media Minimum Age regime tells platforms to take “reasonable steps” to block users under 16 from holding accounts. Platforms design their own layered compliance strategies since regulators leave the technical choices to them. The Age Assurance Technology Trial, published in mid-2025, found that official document verification locks out homeless individuals and some Indigenous communities who lack formal identification. Australian regulators responded by banning government ID as the sole verification method. That decision acknowledged the discriminatory weight that ID requirements place on people who exist outside formal documentation systems.

The Privacy Act 1988 imposes strict data handling rules on Australian operators. Companies must destroy verification inputs like scanned IDs and selfies the moment the check finishes. They retain only binary tokens as output. That approach to data minimization runs against the grain of US and UK frameworks, where retention incentives reward companies for keeping verification records on file. Australian regulators have documented that facial age estimation algorithms produce higher error rates for certain demographic groups. Platforms match their surveillance levels to the actual harm potential of specific content types. Australian regulators rolled out multi-factor age verification for adult content services in December 2025 and opened a new front. Fourth-generation facial biometric tools use AI to predict neuropsychological traits from facial images, including mental health risks and political affiliation. Australian privacy law has no rules covering that category of data.

Lawmakers in each country made different bets about the tradeoff between safety and state authority. US legislators chose flexibility and broad surveillance, and they accepted locked-out citizens as collateral damage. UK regulators centralized control and wrote strict data protection standards, then watched users route around the barriers with VPNs. Australian officials built their system on evidence and privacy-by-design principles, and they still face friction as verification requirements grow into new areas of digital life. The architects of all three systems treated age verification as a technical fix for a social problem. They underestimated the human rights costs and overstated the protective benefits.

They rely on facial age estimation systems. Software engineers program these tools to measure geometric facial features and estimate a user’s age. Criminals exploit this permanence to commit identity theft and fraudulent impersonation for decades. Researchers document bias in facial recognition algorithms. Auditors find high error rates during scans of women and people with darker skin tones. Gatekeepers use these errors to block access to lawful content. Legislators left massive holes in the rules governing new technologies. Surveys from research firms show consumers rank AI-generated fake IDs and deepfakes as bigger threats than the old-school identity theft they grew up worrying about. Consumers keep losing faith in digital identity systems. People refuse to hand over government IDs. And lawmakers keep passing laws that demand the same thing. Australia’s Privacy Act ignores fourth-generation facial biometric data. Systems use AI to extract neuropsychological traits and political affiliation from a photograph of your face. The country plans to roll out multi-factor age verification in December 2025. Australian regulators wrote zero rules to cover the biometric data that rollout will collect. Most governments around the world choose to build verification tools that collect and store personal data in bulk.
