### **Introduction**

Mobs choked the Atlanta streets outside the Fulton County courthouse during the 1913 Leo Frank trial. Jewish lawyers and civic leaders in New York established the Anti-Defamation League that exact year. The founders wrote a mission statement and promised to halt the defamation of Jewish people while securing fair treatment for all citizens. ADL employees spent decades building a reputation as a civil rights powerhouse that politicians and reporters treated as the primary source on domestic hate groups. The staff ran a hidden shadow operation. Employees compiled dossiers on Arab-American activists and antiwar organizers. They hid this work from donors while sharing intelligence files with Israeli government contacts and operatives planted informants inside the Black Panthers and the American Arab Anti-Discrimination Committee.

Management folded criticism of Israeli policy into the working definition of antisemitism. Journalists investigated these activities in the 1970s and uncovered a massive paper trail. Prosecutors exposed the operation during a 1993 San Francisco court case when they produced boxes of stolen police records that operatives took from ADL filing cabinets. And whistleblowers confirmed the document theft. Jewish community members from Brooklyn to Los Angeles raised objections. ADL leadership ignored those complaints. Operatives built domestic spying networks rivaling the reach of a municipal intelligence unit. The legal team tangled with city budgets and federal lobbying disclosure requirements while directors shifted political alliances. Management aligned with hawkish factions in Washington and directors distanced the organization from progressive Jewish groups. Trainers taught counterterrorism courses to local police departments. Staff members sat across the table from Silicon Valley executives writing content moderation rules. You read the 1913 charter and see a clear promise. The board allowed decades of operational habits to drift from that founding language. Directors created a credibility problem the communications team can’t manage.

### **The Architecture of Covert Intelligence: Decades of Surveillance Operations**

ADL officials built an extensive private surveillance network over several decades. ADL operatives used this intelligence apparatus to track political and civil rights organizations across the country while dedicated personnel managed the program and relied on a web of law enforcement collaborators. Roy E. Bullock anchored the operation. The ADL hired him in the 1950s and kept him on the payroll until the 1990s and colleagues called him Cal. Working as the primary investigator, he infiltrated Arab-American groups and anti-apartheid activists while tracking gay radical collectives and Communist cells. Bullock favored invasive tactics and attended restricted events under the alias Elmer Fink. Investigators gleaned extra intel from garbage searches. He ran a phone surveillance system called Operation Eavesdrop and compiled a database holding files on an estimated 12,000 individuals and over 950 organizations. Some records show a lower count of 9,876 people. His targets included the ACLU and Greenpeace. ACT UP and the United Farm Workers Union faced the same scrutiny and the National Lawyers Guild joined their ranks. He spied on Congress members Nancy Pelosi and Ron Dellums. Pete McCloskey appeared in his files.

ADL directors expanded the operation across the country. ADL directors placed undercover operatives in San Francisco and Los Angeles while handlers in Chicago and St. Louis managed additional teams. Handlers in Atlanta and New York managed more teams while Washington D.C. operatives completed the network. The teams used codenames to coordinate activities. Chicago agents went by CHI-3 and St. Louis agents used IRONSIDES. Atlanta handlers relied on FLIPPER while other cells adopted SCOUT and SCUMBAG. Operatives calling themselves HOT SPURS completed the roster. Bullock worked from the West Coast hub with Thomas Gerard, a former CIA agent and inspector in the San Francisco Police Department intelligence unit. Gerard provided confidential law enforcement data and pulled driver license records alongside criminal histories. Gerard supplied Social Security numbers and mug shots to fill the rest of the requests while he added fingerprint cards and vehicle registration information to complete the data grabs. Gerard accessed this material through the SFPD C.A.B.L.E. and CLETS databases. Detectives found police computer logs confirming he conducted 22 queries without authorization on individuals outside formal investigations and this action violated departmental protocols. The men continued the collaboration after the SFPD disbanded its intelligence unit in 1990. Department commanders ordered the destruction of all files. Gerard transferred these classified materials to Bullock, who digitized the records into his personal computer system. Police found evidence during raids on ADL offices showing the organization obtained 75 percent of the information through illegal means.

ADL directors hid their money trail to keep their hands clean. Management paid investigator Roy Bullock five hundred fifty dollars a week. Management refused to route the cash through the official payroll. Beverly Hills attorney Bruce Hochman ran a secret bank account under the fake name L. Patterson. Hochman prosecuted federal cases before taking this private work. He laundered one hundred sixty-nine thousand three hundred seventy-five dollars through that hidden account over eight years. Directors used this arrangement to secure perfect cover. They pleaded ignorance regarding Bullock while they read his files and directed his routine work. David Gurvitz ran the Los Angeles office as a fact-finding librarian. He built thick dossiers on Arab Americans who held anti-Israel leanings. Gurvitz took a confidential FBI report on the Nation of Islam. He fed the pages into a shredder. ADL editors printed those exact secrets in the Washington Times. You don’t miss the government pipeline in that single transaction. Gurvitz spent his career tracking political activists across the sprawling suburbs of Southern California. He traded favors with three Los Angeles sheriff deputies and one San Diego intelligence officer.

Operatives pushed the spy network across oceans. Bullock and Gerard sold reports on anti-apartheid activism to agents from the apartheid-era South African Bureau of State Security, called BOSS. The men met in San Francisco hotels and exchanged paper bags stuffed with new hundred-dollar bills. They collected sixteen thousand dollars over several years. Anne Poirier ran a scholarship program and found that a South African agent bought her personal data for fifteen thousand dollars. Her charity work lacked any Middle East connection. Investigators uncovered stacks of court documents. These files proved the organization shared intelligence with the Mossad and Israeli government officials. National Director Benjamin Epstein wrote a letter in 1961 acknowledging a steady habit of monitoring Arab diplomatic missions. Epstein sent his gathered files to the United States State Department and the Israeli government. Executive Director Leonard Zakim distributed an internal memo in 1992 that named pro-Arab sympathizers on college campuses. He listed Edward Said and Noam Chomsky. Zakim asked his readers to submit extra names to help build thick dossiers on Israel critics. Leaders let these foreign relationships ruin the domestic nonprofit illusion and turned the group into an extension of the Israeli state.

### **Legal Reckoning and Institutional Impunity: Scandals and Settlements**

Journalists exposed the Anti-Defamation League surveillance network and triggered a wave of lawsuits and public scandals. The organization paid settlements and dodged criminal accountability. Investigators established a clear pattern of institutional impunity in the aftermath. The San Francisco District Attorney sent investigators to execute search warrants in April 1993. They hit ADL offices in both San Francisco and Los Angeles while raiding the homes of operatives Roy Bullock and Thomas Gerard. Investigators removed banker boxes stuffed with confidential police reports and arrest records. Those cardboard boxes held fingerprint cards alongside booking photos. The ADL broke multiple California Penal Codes covering computer fraud and criminal justice data misuse. Operatives pulled unauthorized Department of Motor Vehicles records. And the FBI joined the hunt. Federal agents raided ADL offices to investigate a potential criminal conspiracy. Agents seized internal files proving the ADL maintained complete access to the Bullock database. Leadership claimed ignorance. Prosecutors used the seized documents to destroy that defense.

Prosecutors held felony evidence against the ADL. The organization sidestepped all criminal prosecution despite the physical evidence. Leadership signed a settlement with the San Francisco District Attorney in November 1993. Leadership accepted a permanent injunction banning the ADL from obtaining information from state employees bound by confidentiality laws. The ADL paid $75,000 to a hate crime prevention fund and gave $25,000 to a Hate Crimes Reward Fund. They allocated another $25,000 for legal training and sent the final $25,000 to a community relations project. The ADL didn’t admit any wrongdoing. Thomas Gerard accepted a misdemeanor conviction for access without authorization to a police computer system. A judge handed him three years of probation and 45 days of community service. Gerard wrote a check for a $2,500 fine. The judge ignored the sheer scale of the espionage operation when determining this punishment. Roy Bullock ran the spy ring and kept his job while he avoided all criminal charges.

Congressman Pete McCloskey filed a class-action lawsuit against the ADL in 1993 to represent the surveilled individuals and groups. His legal team alleged a sweeping spying operation invaded their privacy. Abdeen Jabara represented the American-Arab Anti-Discrimination Committee in a second federal suit and accused the ADL of handing surveillance data to Mossad. The litigants spent years in court before reaching private agreements. The ADL paid $175,000 for plaintiff legal costs and gave $25,000 to a Jewish-Arab relations foundation in 1996 while keeping its liability denials intact. Jeffrey Blankfort and his co-plaintiffs accepted $50,000 each on February 22, 2002. The ADL lawyers invoked journalistic privilege to shield their sources from public scrutiny. The 1st District Court of Appeal upheld that defense in 1998 and forced plaintiffs to drop their cases because they lacked access to internal ADL communications.

The plaintiffs created a different legal problem with a 1994 defamation lawsuit. ADL operatives advised neighbors to record private telephone conversations via a police scanner and log the details. The neighbors handed those tape recordings to lawyers for a federal civil lawsuit. A jury found the ADL liable and awarded the plaintiffs $10 million in damages in 2000. The appellate judges upheld the verdict and forced the organization to pay the original award plus interest in 2004. It’s rare for the group to pay financial penalties of this magnitude.

Critics demanded criminal charges for the espionage activities. They compared the organization to COINTELPRO and sought the revocation of its tax-exempt status because these activists viewed the group as an arm of the Israeli government. Senior politicians stalled the investigation behind closed doors. An FBI agent wrote a memo on March 29, 1993 detailing the trip two Israeli generals took to Washington to lobby Attorney General Janet Reno. They wanted her to halt the investigation into Bullock and Gerard. The FBI dropped the case and handed prosecution to the San Francisco district attorney while observers called this maneuver a cover-up. So the ADL continued its operations from the same offices. Abraham Foxman served as president during the scandal and kept his position until 2015. The board gave him a $1.5 million retirement package and the leadership insulated itself from the fallout. The organization paid cash to settle lawsuits and walked away free.

### **The Conflation of Criticism and Antisemitism: Advocacy, Data, and the Wikipedia Conflict**

The Anti-Defamation League folds criticism of Israel into its definition of antisemitism. Observers watch the group politicize bigotry. ADL directors champion the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance framework, a document equating specific critiques of Israeli policy with hate speech. The framework lists denying Jewish self-determination and applying double standards to the country as offenses. The text classifies comparing Israeli actions to Nazi Germany as an offense. Activists argue this broad net catches pro-Palestinian rallies and boycott campaigns. ADL staff tag these events as hate crimes. Lobbyists for the organization spend thousands of dollars pressing city councils and university boards to adopt this exact wording.

ADL staff alter the data with this methodology. Hamas attacked Israel on October 7, 2023, and the ADL changed its counting rules the following day. Staffers started classifying protests featuring anti-Zionist chants as antisemitic incidents. And they included rallies organized by Jewish Voice for Peace and IfNotNow in these tallies. ADL managers produced a 360 percent increase in reported hate crimes during late 2023 by applying the new rules. ADL analysts tallied 3,283 incidents following the October attack. ADL executives acknowledged in January 2024 that two-thirds of those reports involved the Gaza war. The directors placed 1,317 reclassified anti-Zionist expressions in this total. Journalists reject the methodology. Reporters at The New York Times and PBS publish ADL numbers and omit the counting changes because they can’t reconcile the math. A Times reporter published a 2024 article detailing a record high in antisemitic incidents. The writer skipped the part about anti-Israel protests. ADL directors published their annual Audit of Antisemitic Incidents and listed 9,354 events in 2024. Policymakers use this audit to draft legislation. Critics see a transparent push for a pro-Israel narrative.

The volunteer editors running the English Wikipedia reached a consensus in June 2024 and deemed the Anti-Defamation League a “generally unreliable” source for the Israeli-Palestinian conflict. The community relegated the organization to the same tier as Newsmax and the National Enquirer. These editors spent weeks reviewing evidence. The reviewers found the group operates as a pro-Israel advocacy organization and conflates criticism of Israel with antisemitism. The volunteers pointed to the revised methodology the group adopted after October 7 2023. They flagged public statements from CEO Jonathan Greenblatt. Greenblatt compared the kaffiyeh to the Nazi swastika and labeled student protesters as campus proxies for Iran. A near-consensus among the editors supports deeming the ADL unreliable on antisemitism topics. The community anticipates a formal declaration. Editors permit the use of the ADL for narrow topics like the hate symbol database. The community prohibits citations for information about living persons. The volunteers based this ruling on prior decisions they made against Fox News for politics and science coverage. They restricted NGO Monitor for pro-Israel bias.

The organization launched a counter-offensive after losing credibility on a major platform. The ADL Center for Technology and Society published a sprawling report on March 18 2025 alleging a coordinated campaign by at least 30 Wikipedia editors to insert anti-Israel and antisemitic narratives into articles. The editors removed citations to reputable sources. They promoted criticism of Israel and downplayed Palestinian violence. The authors claimed these editors produced double the output of comparable groups and 18 times the volume in group communications. The authors cited specific edits. One editor changed the “violence and terrorism” subhead on the Hamas page to “violence” while another omitted the death toll from the October 7 attacks. The group welcomed disciplinary actions on June 4 2025. The Wikipedia arbitration committee punished editors Iskandar323 and Selfstudier, alongside Nableezy, for participating in an “anti-Israel bad-faith editing campaign”. The organization called for systemic reforms. The Wikimedia Foundation responded with a preliminary review and found the ADL conclusions “troubling and flawed”. The foundation asserted that editors must present content with strict neutrality. You see a central tension in this battle. The ADL seeks to control the narrative on digital platforms by discrediting opposing viewpoints as antisemitic. Independent communities like Wikipedia resist an attempt to manipulate information and suppress dissent.

### **Financial Structure, Governance, and Political Realignment**

The Anti-Defamation League runs a sprawling financial operation. The main entity and the Anti-Defamation League Foundation hold the purse strings. Accountants logged $204,135,944 in net assets on June 30, 2024, dwarfing the $31 million from 2022. Auditors reported $261,508,202 in total assets. The foundation held $116,704,770 in endowment funds. Donors fund the enterprise. They supplied 98.8 percent of the $38.3 million revenue for the fiscal year closing in June 2023. And those same donors provided 97.7 percent of the $103 million revenue in the previous fiscal year closing in December 2022. The group pads the ledger with investment income and asset sales, generating 51.7 percent of its 2015 revenue from those asset sales. The organization raised $91 million and spent $70 million in 2020. So the group kept a $21 million surplus. The group applied for a $7.4 million Paycheck Protection Program loan, and the government forgave that debt in July 2021.

Executives take a huge slice of the budget. The board increased CEO Jonathan Greenblatt’s compensation from $382,523 in 2018 to $1,251,732 in 2021. They added a $159,000 bonus to that 2021 package. He pocketed $1,207,978 in 2022. Greenblatt collected $6 million in total pay between 2017 and 2022, while Frederic Bloch pocketed $518,700 in 2022. Steven Sheinberg collected $325,211 and Adam Neufeld earned $302,780 that year. Abraham Foxman holds the National Director Emeritus title, and the organization paid him $81,275 each year from 2020 to 2022 for zero hours of work per week. The ADL reimbursed Greenblatt for gym memberships. The group paid for first-class travel and charter flights while keeping the details private, and they covered companion trips. The organization hires independent contractors. The group paid First International Resources $1.7 million for public relations in 2020. The ADL paid CDR Fundraising Group $1.5 million and gave Situation Interactive $700,000 for media work.

Jonathan Greenblatt steers the ADL through a sweeping political pivot. Greenblatt used the October 7 Hamas attack as an opening to equate anti-Zionism with antisemitism, and his staff targets student groups like Students for Justice in Palestine and Jewish Voice for Peace. Greenblatt calls these organizations radical left entities comparable to neo-Nazis. Staffers revolted against the new agenda. Seventeen former employees and board members quit in 2024 over mission drift. Workers say managers ordered them to quiet student protests over Gaza, and the ADL stopped offering broad anti-bias education. Greenblatt scrapped the general school programs in December 2023. Greenblatt scrubbed the ADL website by June 2025 and deleted the voting rights and racial justice pages to shrink the organizational footprint.

Greenblatt pushed this realignment into the political arena and aligned the group with the Trump administration in 2023. He attended a June 2025 meeting with Trump officials to discuss a new Muslim ban. ADL leaders condemned this exact policy in the past as a dark and shameful stain, and Greenblatt ignored the renewed ban. He hired the Republican lobbying firm Ballard Partners in June 2025 to build ties with MAGA leaders. Greenblatt spent $1.4 million on federal lobbying in 2024 to push legislation defining anti-Zionism as antisemitism. Longtime donor Walter Jospin and regional board member Steven Ludwig resigned in protest. The Jospin family gave one million dollars to the cause. National Education Association members voted in July 2025 to sever ties, and union leaders dismissed the ADL as a fraudulent educational partner. Greenblatt altered the core identity of the organization.

### **Influence on Law Enforcement, Media, and Public Policy**

The Anti-Defamation League spent decades building influence over law enforcement and public policy. It branded itself as the definitive source on extremism and used that reputation to steer institutional behavior and media coverage. The FBI became its deepest partner. The two organizations co-developed training programs for years. FBI Director James Comey spoke at the ADL’s National Leadership Summit in 2014 and delivered what attendees called a “love letter to the ADL.” He mandated the organization’s Law Enforcement and Society training for all incoming agents. And the pair produced a Hate Crimes Training Manual together. The FBI came to depend on ADL intelligence. The organization fed the bureau more than 2,700 tips in 2024, according to published accounts. Patel ended that relationship in 2025. The FBI director severed all ties, accusing the ADL of running “disgraceful ops spying on Americans” and operating as a “political front masquerading as a watchdog.” Right-wing critics pushed back against the ADL’s labeling of conservative groups, and Patel cited that pressure when he cut ties. Other federal agencies took note, and his public accusations cost the ADL credibility inside law enforcement circles.

The ADL built a pipeline for exchanges between U.S. and Israeli law enforcement. Its National Counter-Terrorism Seminar flies U.S. police officials to Israel for counterterrorism training. ICE leadership and US Marshals officials have attended, along with brass from the Naval Criminal Investigative Service. Opponents charge that the program imports tactics from the occupied Palestinian territories and uses them to militarize U.S. police. Activists killed the trips in Vermont and Durham, North Carolina. ADL staff recommended shutting the program down entirely in a 2020 internal memo. But the organization kept it alive with a revised curriculum instead. The ADL teamed up with neoconservative groups like the Jewish Institute for National Security of America to run parallel exchange programs. George Selim, the ADL’s director of anti-bias and security programs, champions the Countering Violent Extremism initiative. Selim ran the same program at the Department of Homeland Security before joining the ADL. Critics say the initiative targeted Muslim communities at disproportionate rates.

Beat reporters treat the Anti-Defamation League as absolute gospel. Newsrooms at The New York Times and CNN print the data and ignore the underlying math on the page. ADL staff dominate coverage of white supremacists. Writers cite the group in two out of three stories about these extremist factions, and editors gave the organization a huge platform in 2019. Editors increased mentions of the group fifteen hundred percent that single year. Silicon Valley executives handed over the keys to content moderation. The ADL joins the YouTube Trusted Flagger program and flags videos for removal. Staffers decide which uploads qualify as hate speech and hold direct power over digital censorship. Executives at Facebook and Twitter bowed to similar pressure regarding cyberhate initiatives. Programmers built the H.E.A.T. Map to track extremism. Researchers pull raw numbers from this interactive tool and paste them into their own reports. Wikipedia editors imposed a hard ceiling on this reach. They reviewed the organization and banned it as a reliable source for its core topics.

ADL lobbyists shape public policy from the inside. They pushed governments and universities to adopt the IHRA definition of antisemitism. Staffers advocated for the Matthew Shepard and James Byrd Jr. Hate Crimes Prevention Act in 2009 and wrote a model hate crimes statute. Lawmakers in forty-five states and the District of Columbia passed that exact text. ADL lobbyists reject bills that weaken hate crime laws. Florida House Bill 11 proposed adding homeless status to protected categories. And lobbyists killed it. ADL executives bought JLens to rate companies on their Israel support, and they used this acquisition to enter economic advocacy. They pressure firms to oppose boycotts of Israel. Lobbyists use this web of influence to define hate and train law enforcement officers. ADL staff instruct reporters on covering extremism. Leaders cement their authority with this feedback loop.

### **The Erosion of Trust: Institutional Credibility in the 21st Century**

ADL staffers spent years tracking citizens. The employees blended political dissent with antisemitism to manipulate digital narratives and ruined their institutional credibility. You’d see the ADL as a top civil rights organization. And internal staff question that legitimacy. Wikipedia volunteers cemented this reputational damage in 2024 and the editors voted to label the ADL unreliable for Israeli-Palestinian conflict topics. The volunteers reviewed a documented pattern of misrepresentation. They stripped the organization of its factual authority on a volatile geopolitical dispute. Shira Klein saw this bias in print. ADL writers cited her work in a 2025 report and Klein noted the publication had “everything to do with controlling the narrative about Israel.” She exposed an outfit that puts advocacy over facts.

Union bosses and university deans reject the group. National Education Association (NEA) leaders represent millions of educators and the union president severed all ties with the ADL in 2025. The president declared the group an untrustworthy educational partner. Teachers ran the “Drop the ADL From Schools” campaign before this split and the organizers argued the ADL produced biased materials unsuitable for classrooms. And federal agents cut ties. FBI Director Kash Patel terminated a decades-long partnership in 2025 and accused the ADL of running “disgraceful ops spying on Americans.” The director rejected the group and ruined their standing as a security advisor.

Jonathan Greenblatt sits in the corner office while his employees and financial backers walk out the door. Seventeen individuals cut ties with the organization before staff members boiled over in a loud January 2024 revolt. Workers rejected his specific brand of leadership and demanded a new direction for the civil rights group. High-profile employees quit over the pro-Israel advocacy during the Gaza war and left scathing exit interviews. One senior manager called the comparison of anti-Israel protesters to white supremacists a dishonest and damaging claim. You watch the polished public posture of the ADL on television. You read the private beliefs of the people doing the daily work in their resignation letters. Greenblatt forces a rigid public posture that contradicts the private beliefs of his workers.

Greenblatt published a 2025 report attacking Wikipedia editors to regain lost ground and he failed. Critics see a group that focuses on silencing opponents and ignores good-faith dialogue with its detractors. Greenblatt aligned the group with elements of the Trump administration by staying silent on a proposed Muslim ban and hiring a Republican lobbying firm. He alienated the liberal base that gave money to the group for decades with these specific political choices. Observers point out the danger of defending figures like Elon Musk after he performed a Nazi salute in public. They argue Greenblatt enables the exact bigotry the founders wanted to fight and puts Jewish safety at severe risk. Greenblatt uses the vast financial resources of the organization to maintain a massive physical footprint across the country. He killed the core reputation of the group as a neutral defender of civil rights with those exact choices. Greenblatt runs an influential institution with deep governmental and media ties in Washington. The communities he claims to serve question his reliability and ignore his press releases. He must reconcile his current advocacy mission with the free speech principles the founders established to survive in this job.

### **Conclusion**

Directors at the Anti-Defamation League built a civil rights powerhouse and spent thirty years running covert operations that contradicted their public charter. Investigators uncovered a domestic spy ring in the 1990s. They proved operatives monitored thousands of citizens and supplied intelligence to apartheid South Africa and Israeli authorities. Plaintiffs secured financial settlements over those operations. Prosecutors didn’t file criminal charges against the executives who ran the surveillance network. Board members aligned their advocacy with a pro-Israel political agenda during the current century. And staff members redefined antisemitism to include anti-Zionist speech alongside pro-Palestinian activism. Wikipedia editors designated the group as unreliable on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict. Those volunteers stripped the organization of institutional credibility and forced the board to retreat. Police chiefs fractured their partnerships with the group over the years and withdrew their officers from joint training seminars. FBI officials severed ties in 2025 and closed the door on future collaboration. You can see the influence evaporate in real time. Executives retain substantial financial reserves and command a pervasive media presence. Critics reject the core claim of neutral arbitration over hate and bigotry. Directors turned a trusted civil rights defender into a polarizing institution through domestic surveillance operations and politicized speech definitions. They won’t regain their old standing anytime soon.
