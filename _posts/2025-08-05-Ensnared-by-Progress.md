### **Introduction**

You hear dread and wonder in public conversations about technology. People look at the hardware surrounding them and see engines of progress while they view software as neutral artifacts serving human needs. But a heavy tradition of thought argues that machines form the physical environment defining humanity. You find this argument spanning dense sociological theory and crude anarcho-primitivist pamphlets. Jacques Ellul laid this foundation in his book *The Technological Society* before Theodore Kaczynski expanded the argument in *Industrial Society and Its Future*. Kaczynski advanced the critique in *Anti-Tech Revolution* and directed his attack at the entire trajectory of civilization. We can’t avoid deciding if we hold a simple tool or a new species of power running on strict logic. Tech expansion won’t spare human autonomy and the ecological web. People don’t have much time to understand and stop this machine before the damage becomes permanent.

### **Technique Versus Technology**

Jacques Ellul wrote *The Technological Society* to map a specific colonization of modern life. He ignored metal boxes and microchips to track a new principle. Humans seek efficiency and organization in their routines. You see this drive in boardrooms and factory floors. Ellul defines “technique” as the collection of methods humans invent to achieve absolute efficiency across human activity. “Technique” is a value system.

Managers subordinate organic traditions and spiritual beliefs to method and calculation. You watch a general drill a regiment and notice the exact same routines in classrooms and corporate offices. Engineers build artificial systems to replace natural circumstances. They pour thick concrete over the soil.

Workers abandon human intention to chase efficiency. A supervisor scraps old protocols. That same supervisor demands new ones to outpace the competition. Inventors stack new tools on top of old tools. They build heavier machines and write denser code. The engineers force the next upgrade.

Planners pull distinct fields of life into a single technical system. You visit two rival nations and find identical power grids and matching supply chains in both capitals. System administrators achieve critical mass and ignore elected officials. They treat citizens as raw material. You process living humans as machine inputs.

Jiang expands this exact reading in the essay *The Technological Society as Paradise and Dystopia*. Radical critics borrow these themes to build their own arguments.

### **From Utopian to Dystopian**

Edward Bellamy published *Looking Backward* in the late nineteenth century and imagined an industrial society where managers applied strict logic to generate wealth and free time for the workers. And Jiang spends pages on this specific book in his thesis. Bellamy demanded strict economic equality for all citizens. He outlined a machine-like order in his blueprint and the directors flattened personal quirks to force citizens into the exact same mold. You lose the messy parts of being alive because the architects don’t leave any room for error in their frictionless environment. They engineered the rebellion out of the human brain. You can’t support yourself outside the grid and the wardens handle outliers through public shaming or physical force. Jiang points to page 50 for the grim details.

Yevgeny Zamyatin wrote *We* to puncture the utopian dream. He imagined a society where the rulers removed spontaneity and irrationality through surgery. Zamyatin watched the early Soviet leaders push their mechanical ideology and he argued that citizens refuse to submit and start a perpetual revolution. The rebels channel raw energy and burst through the concrete walls the builders erect to contain them. And the guards won’t loosen their relentless and strict control over the population. Jacques Ellul noticed the exact same universal trap while he conducted his own extensive research. The planners reject all differences when they deploy an overpowering system.

Ellul summarized the trap and Jiang read his work with care. The French thinker believed that citizens abandon moral and political objections once the technicians colonize a society. Humans turn into a circulating pool of means and ends. The directors subordinate human creativity to pure efficiency and absolute control. Ellul saw zero mechanisms for people to regain power once the directors mature the process.

### **The Human Animal in a Labyrinth**

Kaczynski built his entire framework around a psychological baseline he called the “power process”, a primal drive you see in mammals chasing prey across a plain. Humans take that exact urge and channel it into setting physical targets and exerting sweat equity to hit those marks, an effort that gives you meaning and dignity. Industrial society breaks this loop. Corporate executives feed you surrogate activities, trading hunting for a desk job and a weekend golf habit. You spend your hours on these distractions while bureaucrats use the vast footprint of the corporate machine to strip you of your independence.

You suffer severe boredom and clinical depression. You feel the stress mounting in your chest every morning as the alarm clock rings, but doctors and hospital administrators intervene with psychiatric appointments and pill bottles to numb the pain. These medical professionals prescribe chemical treatments to make you compliant with corporate demands. You get to choose your brand of toothpaste and your streaming service, and you exercise that freedom. Corporate managers grant you these trivial choices to mask your total captivity.

You watch your family ties and neighborhood bonds dissolve over the years. You pledge your allegiance to massive conglomerates because the CEOs of these corporate behemoths possess their own ruthless survival instincts. Factory owners feed you because a starving workforce stops the assembly line and ruins their profit margins. Factory owners offer you corporate wellness seminars because a depressed workforce stops buying products, meaning the board of directors prioritizes its own operational continuity over your personal well-being.

Kaczynski and Ellul saw the dirt and the smog hanging over the valley. Politicians and CEOs chase maximum yield and pave the wilderness for corporate profits while corporations break the natural feedback loops of the planet. Plant managers pump toxins into the river and engineers invent a new filtration technology to clean the river. The new filtration equipment produces its own toxic byproducts. You inherit acreage buried under synthetic waste and middle managers trying to micromanage the biosphere. They fail.

### **Impossibility of Rational Control and the Myth of Reform**

Jacques Ellul and Theodore Kaczynski spent decades writing about human failure to steer our collective future, treating the whole situation like a grim religious doctrine and demanding absolute obedience from their readers. You look at human groups. Planners draft elaborate blueprints for reform and generate bizarre consequences.

Kaczynski laid out specific rules in his manifesto to explain how citizens experience time as a blind drift while people ride feedback loops they fail to measure. Leaders enact grand shifts in public behavior and watch the changes disappear. Those leaders build volatile new structures from the wreckage.

Reformers push green technology or gentler social programs to fix the world, expecting a parade and a pat on the back for their good intentions. Bureaucrats absorb these pushes but multiply the original problems.

You read the opening chapter of his manifesto and find a sprawling graveyard of arrogant schemes and trace centuries of hubris and misplaced optimism. Roman emperors banned luxury goods while modern politicians bulldoze entire neighborhoods. Both groups failed to fix human behavior.

Analysts hit a massive wall of chaos and tangled logic when forecasting human progress, even when they possess infinite computing power and access to global data streams. So you can’t step outside the machine you want to map, and you alter what people do today when you publish a forecast about tomorrow. Kaczynski noted this paradox on page sixteen of his text.

Ellul and Kaczynski dismissed political labels as mere window dressing because engineers prioritize raw technical output over political beliefs when they design the infrastructure of modern life. You vote for a democracy and the builders construct the same power grids. Activists pass new regulations to fix the grid and the builders absorb those regulations to protect their blueprints.

### **The Social Management of Dissent**

Theodore Kaczynski details a mechanism for social control in his manuscript “Technological Slavery” starting on page 190 and labels this concept “The System’s Neatest Trick.” Bureaucrats face unrest. And they redirect that rebellion to serve their own survival. It’s a playbook investigators find in leaked corporate memos where middle managers filter opposition into approved grievances like animal rights and environmental justice.

Civic leaders turn these issues into institutionalized forms of resistance and shareholders benefit from this arrangement. HR directors use anti-discrimination policies to incorporate more workers into the corporate machine. Shift supervisors keep the factory floor busy.

Communications teams use integration propaganda to instill docility and lawfulness while government handlers use agitation propaganda to generate emotional calls to action on safe issues. Power brokers marginalize or co-opt any movement threatening foundational power. These brokers strip these groups of their teeth. So citizens move on.

Kaczynski identifies universities as centers of domesticated dissent. It’s a role tenured professors embrace. They domesticate radical thought and reduce revolution to reformist causes while deans focus on leftist identity politics and multiculturalism. These deans bolster the technical society through these initiatives. Alumni staff and equip the machine they study. Students keep the campus quad quiet. Trustees grow the endowment.

### **The Irreversibility of Technical Progress and the Illusion of Choice**

Jacques Ellul and Ted Kaczynski documented the forward march of new tools across decades of dense archival research. The physical infrastructure fuses the obvious drawbacks with the promised benefits. And you accept the whole package. Engineers pushing a breakthrough in microchip fabrication drag global shipping and warehouse logistics along for the ride, binding distinct industries into a single rigid mesh. The machine moves in one direction.

Corporations pushing widespread adoption strip away consumer choice. Towns and supply chains mandate the use of new systems to keep the local economy running, and residents remain dependent on the grid if they want to pay a mortgage. Only a handful of eccentrics manage to abandon the grid and sell their houses to move off the map. Kaczynski outlined this exact trap in section 127 of his manifesto and detailed the illusion of the modern marketplace. Shoppers treat a new gadget as a voluntary purchase, and managers use the tool to alter the social fabric and rewire the job market until citizens lose the option. Employees adopt the software to buy groceries.

### **Autonomy, Dignity, and What Remains of “Freedom”**

Jacques Ellul and Ted Kaczynski treated freedom as the raw power to steer your own existence and dictate your daily routines. You control your survival. You secure your meals and build your alliances while the state pushes you toward pre-approved channels. These two writers rejected that compromise and demanded absolute authority over life-and-death stakes.

The managers running modern technical societies shrink that definition to a simple shopping trip. Corporate executives hand you a massive catalog of consumer gadgets and streaming services to browse. You pick a brand and select a weekend hobby. The architects of this arrangement keep the fundamental trajectory of your life locked inside their own boardrooms.

Government officials and private executives hoard new mechanisms to shrink your autonomy. They deploy vast camera networks across cities and rewrite your DNA. Engineers build thinking machines to map the human brain and predict your next purchase. These bureaucrats mold your internal thoughts into a documented reality. You can read the blueprint in sections 147 through 160 of the ISAIF filings.

### **AI: Efficiency, Autonomy, and the Domestication of Human Agency**
Ellul and Kaczynski share a single premise. Technology operates as a system with its own momentum, autonomous, self-propagating, closer to a living process than a hammer or a printing press. Machines don’t wait for someone to switch them on. Biotechnology and modern AI show this better than anything that came before them.

For Ellul, this drive has a name: technique. He defined it as the pursuit of “absolute efficiency by any method” [(Ellul, The Technological Society, Ch. 3)](https://archive.org/details/JacquesEllulTheTechnologicalSociety), applied without distinguishing between a social problem and a personal one. AI meets that definition better than anything that came before it. It doesn’t sit on a shelf waiting to be picked up. It optimizes data, optimizes labor, optimizes process, optimizes the shape of human interaction, and it doesn’t stop. Ellul mapped out the paths this kind of expansion takes. AI walks every single one.

Take autonomization first. Machine learning models learn on their own and adjust on their own, and each adjustment buries the process a little deeper. Ellul had a name for this too: the self-driving quality of technique. Its logic outruns anyone’s ability to predict where it lands, let alone steer it. Ask an engineer why a large model produced a given output and you’ll often get a shrug dressed up in technical language. That’s the black box effect, and it’s what autonomization looks like from the inside.

Then there’s ubiquity. AI has become infrastructure, the way electricity did before it, threaded through the systems people rely on without a second thought. It sits inside search engines, inside contract review at law firms, inside the logistics networks that get packages to your door. Opt out, and you fall behind. Ellul predicted this. So did Kaczynski, describing how a technology moves from “once optional, now necessary” without anyone voting on it [(ISAIF, §127)](https://archive.org/details/kaczynski2/mode/2up).

The third pattern hits closer to home. AI now handles the diagnosing, and it composes the text and art that a person used to make with their own hands or head. Kaczynski called that kind of work part of the “power process,” and argued that solving real problems for yourself is where a sense of purpose comes from [(Technological Slavery, “The Power Process”)](https://www.thetedkarchive.com/uploads/tedkarchive/t-k-ted-kaczynski-technological-slavery-1.pdf). Hand that work to a machine and the purpose goes with it. The result resembles the drift Kaczynski associated with surrogate activities: a passive boredom, and underneath it, a low hum of existential unease that doesn’t resolve.

### **Biotechnology**

Jacques Ellul charted artificialization in the twentieth century. You can watch his exact concept unfold inside modern CRISPR labs and drug discovery server rooms. Lab technicians rewrite the fundamental building blocks of breathing organisms, forcing the biological code to yield to their keystrokes.

Programmers dictate physical traits that random mutation governed in previous eras, and the wild environment gives way to manufactured substitutes. Agricultural scientists engineer drought-resistant wheat while medical researchers splice human genes to cure inherited diseases. Engineers swap biological chance for technical blueprints printed on sterile paper.

Ted Kaczynski warned about the blindness of human foresight. Bioethicists debating gene drives and synthetic viruses prove his point. Lab workers take command of evolution and trigger cascading protein misfolds across soil microbiomes. Chapter one of his manifesto details these specific unforeseen consequences with grim precision. Government scientists can’t tame the sheer complexity of root networks despite massive budget increases. Kaczynski built his entire argument around this specific human failure.

State apparatuses extend social control to the molecular level. State clerks use databases and digital vaccine passports to track citizens and dictate their freedom to travel and reproduce across borders. Jacques Ellul argued that system administrators won’t leave people alone, pushing them into the rigid arms of the administrative state. Bureaucrats remove personal choice to guarantee public health. Airlines demand your genetic code before they issue a boarding pass.

### **The Politics of (Non-)Resistance and the Co-Option of Dissent**

Mature technological systems neutralize resistance. Jacques Ellul and Ted Kaczynski documented this pattern decades ago. Look at AI and biotechnology today.

Corporate giants absorb critics who demand ethical rules. Executives turn ethical frameworks into corporate marketing and regulatory rubber stamps. These tools clear the path for expansion. Kaczynski called this phenomenon the system’s neatest trick. Activism demands responsible AI, yet tech firms manage human life without opposition.

And authorities deploy predictive policing and health monitoring under the guise of public safety. You accept surveillance. You accept dependency. You can’t opt out. Participation in society requires compliance with system norms. The old boundary of freedom disappears.

AI and biotechnology merge. The system works as a self-reinforcing web. Machine learning drives synthetic biology and social engineering. Programmers use code to configure these systems. Human expertise won’t matter. The monoculture accepts only measurable elements.

You lose choices. Ellul warned that societies resisting technique become isolated and irrelevant. Societies gain efficiency and lose freedom when they embrace technique.

### **Pathways of Revolution, Collapse, or Adaptation**

Kaczynski repeatedly returns, in ever more analytic and polemical prose, to the conclusion that nothing short of revolution, by which he means the deliberate, organized, violent or at least radically disruptive destruction of the technological/industrial system, has any chance of halting the movement toward ever-greater technical domination. Reform, he says, is consistently outpaced and overwhelmed by technical momentum.

He offers no utopian blueprints: “A new kind of society cannot be designed on paper.” Instead, he offers failed utopias, of the left, of social justice, of “sustainable development”, as warnings, echoing both Ellul and the classics of dystopia. His preferred model is not a new civilization but a renewed world of small, autonomous, face-to-face units, or possibly a regression to nomadic hunting and gathering, which he argues is the only mode of existence historically known to be compatible with autonomy, dignity, and ecological sustainability [(The Truth About Primitive Life, Technological Slavery , p. 126ff)](https://www.thetedkarchive.com/uploads/tedkarchive/t-k-ted-kaczynski-technological-slavery-1.pdf).

### **Final Observations**

Despite the fact that mainstream political opinion treats these arguments as, at best, catastrophist or vestigially “Luddite,” the weight of evidence, historical experience, and lived reality continues to move further in the direction forecast by Ellul, Kaczynski, and their allies. The very technological and institutional tools used to argue for “inclusion,” for “participation,” for the “social good” become, as technical society matures, the instruments by which all participation except that which serves the system is neutralized.

The dominant view emerging from the most forceful critical theorists of technology is not one of optimism. In the thinking of Ellul, Kaczynski, and their successors, the system is self-stabilizing, absorbing, or neutralizing both political and cultural opposition. It is not a simple matter of “wisely using” technology, nor of better regulation, nor of “democratizing” innovation. Technology is not merely a tool, it is a *systemic environment*, with its own logic, inertia, and autonomy.

The final contradiction, never fully resolved by either author, is whether the technical system is fatalistically self-perpetuating or whether it faces a radical, inevitable collapse. But both agree on the deep incompatibility between unbounded technical expansion and any classical sense of human dignity, autonomy, or wildness.

### **Conclusion**

The conclusion is as bleak as it is clear: if revolution is unlikely and reform is even less plausible, then our fate is largely to be subsumed into the needs and logic of the technical society. Whether this ultimately leads to environmental ruin, social anomie, engineered docility, or the replacement of humans by more efficient organisms, the shape of the future is now determined primarily by the runaway train of technological progress.

For those wishing to challenge it, the old optimism about steering technology to good ends looks like a comforting myth, thoroughly outdated by the realities of technique. The deepest forms of resistance, if they exist at all, emerge not from new technologies but from what remains of humanity’s stubborn assertion of autonomy, wildness, and freedom, even as those qualities are rendered increasingly marginal and, perhaps, obsolete.
