Introduction

The “Tranquil Passport” exercise, conducted in late June 2025, marks a watershed in U.S. and international special pathogen response. The multi-phase drill tested the full cycle of responding to international cases of high-consequence infectious diseases (HCID), from airport reception to regional treatment center care, leveraging the first-of-its-kind Portable Biocontainment Unit (PBCU), a technology for multi-patient, long-distance transport with in-transit medical support and infection containment.

Infectious diseases, and evolving biothreats continue to disrupt every dimension of society, supply chains, travel, and economy. Against the backdrop of the COVID-19 pandemic’s aftermath and the anticipated risks surrounding the 2026 FIFA World Cup and other mass events, Tranquil Passport exemplifies the shift toward fast, networked, “whole-of-community” health security.

But behind the headlines is a deeper story: years of scientific and operational deficit, made clear not just by COVID-19, but also by recurring outbreaks of Ebola, Marburg, SARS, and MERS; failures in international cooperation; and a surge in funding/technology innovation. The exercise offers a view on new systems, both biological and bureaucratic, being built and pressure-tested for future shocks.

What Did Tranquil Passport Test?

Scenario:

International “patients” (actors) exposed to a fictional Ebola-like virus are moved from Toronto to Dulles, then to special units at MedStar Washington Hospital Center, Children’s National Hospital, Johns Hopkins Hospital, UNC, Emory University, and others.

Core test: transferring patients from air (jumbo jet) using the PBCU, then secure ambulance transfer, a simultaneous pediatric-adult admission, and a “provider down” scenario (simulating a healthcare worker emergency in full PPE).

Sites rehearsed logistics, decontamination, PPE protocols, unique needs for high-risk groups (pregnant women, children), and chain-of-custody for infection control.

Technical innovation—PBCU:

Isolates up to 10 patients, supports in-transit critical care, keeps crews fully separated from infected patients.

Modular for air and ground: fits C-130, L-100 aircraft, or custom trailers.

Enables long-haul patient movement for multiple concurrent cases, versus slow/limited legacy single-patient pods.

Operational lessons:

Leadership: Joint calls, instant regional coalition activation, and scenario escalation as would occur for a real global spillover event.

Workforce: Mixed teams; both adult and pediatric specialties tested side-by-side.

Security: Airport handoff to ambulance, tailored briefing for local and federal law enforcement, emergency evacuation drills if incident spreads.

Public communications: Media presence, on-site observers from multiple countries (e.g., AUSMAT from Australia, Japan, Taiwan), cross-learning for future international outbreaks.

The National Special Pathogen System (NSPS)

Tranquil Passport's real intent was to put the U.S. National Special Pathogen System (NSPS) through its paces. NSPS is a tiered network of frontline hospitals, healthcare coalitions, and 13 federally funded Regional Emerging Special Pathogen Treatment Centers (RESPTCs) serving as biocontainment hubs (network map and detail ). NSPS links federal, state, and hospital partners through:

National exercises (like Tranquil Passport)

Logistics & data-sharing protocols

On-call rapid deployment teams and infection control monitoring

Dedicated funding via HHS ASPR

NSPS outcomes evident in Tranquil Passport:

Seamless transfers between transport, hospital, and command.

Testing new triage/evacuation/drill protocols for hypothetical “provider down” in PPE.

Validation of cross-hospital, cross-state strategy, critical in real multi-jurisdiction outbreaks.

Major Funding, Governance, and Policy Backdrop

U.S. and International Investment
The exercise and broader infrastructure are supported by rising federal investment and global action:

ASPR FY2025-26 Budget: A $3.8B base, plus $10.5B flex fund for pandemic preparedness and technology (full budget doc ).

Pandemic Fund (World Bank): Over $2.1B pooled from the U.S., EU, Australia, China, Japan, UAE, the Gates/Rockefeller/Wellcome Foundations, etc. (see thepandemicfund.org ).

Rapid third-round grants >$885M in 75 countries.

Directly funds diagnostic readiness, One Health collaborations, early warning, and simulation exercises.

Hospital and System Preparedness

Hospital Preparedness Program (HPP) : Only federal stream for hospital system readiness, supporting coalitions, drills, surge plans, comms systems. Vital for integrating hospitals with public health, EMS, and biocontainment units.

NIOSH ERHMS guidelines : Set out a whole-cycle approach to physical/mental health screening, PPE, credentialing, monitoring, and debrief for staff in deployments.

Global Science, Data, and Innovation Ecosystem

Tranquil Passport’s operational advances rest on wider shifts:

Digital Preparedness
PANDEM-2 dashboard (paper ):

An EU-funded, GDPR-compliant platform for data fusion, risk analytics, scenario modeling, and hospital coordination, built and tested in real cross-border pandemic drills.

Modular, user-customizable, and supports scenario-based stress-testing for health agencies across Europe.

AI and forecasting (Nature review ):

AI/ML now central to outbreak forecasting, triage, contaminated zone mapping, supply demand, genomic variant detection, and more.

Still, translation of advanced modeling into “operational” products lags, most scalable solutions have come from real-world public health–oriented tools built during COVID-19 and after.

OECD and Eu

OECD Smart Spending 2025 :

Tracking GPGs (Global Public Goods) for health, measuring country-by-country investment in PPR: high-income nations spent $122/capita on PPR vs. $2.6 in lower-middle and $3.5 in low-income as of 2022.

Emphasizes the need for resilient, primary care as the base for response, and warns of trade-offs (funding pandemic response often crowds out nutrition/water/basic care).

EU4Health and CEPS governance review : $4.4B (2021-27), with focus on interoperable data, regional stockpiles, supply chain monitoring, digital health integration, and pandemic “stress tests.”

How Measurement, Drills, Simulation Fit In

The Exercise: What Was Actually Measured/Validated?
Hands-on validation of theory: Tranquil Passport is the real-world test for “One Health” policies and ambitious grant guidelines funded by the Pandemic Fund, grounding dashboards, chains of command, and protocol audits in practice.

Monitoring and evaluation (M&E): The Pandemic Fund’s M&E Guidelines mandate after-action reviews; JEE/SPAR/PVS indicators; on-the-ground drills; custom simulation exercises; integration of learning into country capacities.

Global health science and policy lessons: OECD COVID-19, Resilience, and the Interface Between Science, Policy and Society : Emphasizes multisector “meta-learning,” ongoing drills, data-sharing, mutual trust between science and policy, consistent investment post-crisis, and continuous improvement.

The Drill in the Real-World System

From the Fairfax Times coverage :

150+ observers, including delegations/AUSMAT, involved at Dulles.

Direct transfer from air to ambulance; multi-patient isolation simultaneously.

Drill ran across four days and multiple states, with lessons immediately captured for future protocol refinement.

Strengths:

Multi-jurisdiction collaboration is now doctrine, not just theory.

Biocontainment transport goes from being “a future wish” to an operational asset, thanks to years of incremental scientific, engineering, and clinical investment.

Governance, Gaps, and Open Questions

Gaps
Funding volatility: OECD and World Bank data show major spikes and dips in PPR financing. Multiple reports warn of underfunding (in both high and low resource settings), and the risk that new funds (like the Pandemic Fund) are too “donor-dominated” or fragmented to hit systemic, long-term gaps in hospital and cross-border readiness.

Hospital/systemic churn: U.S. forecasts suggest core preparedness streams (like HPP) are under threat from budget/bureaucratic reshuffling, with unclear successor programs (details ).

Cyber-resilience: ASPR warns cyberattacks are increasing; many hospital and health system crises now “begin digitally” (see summary ).

Opportunities and Structural Shifts
Open science/data/collaboration: The emphasis on dashboards (like PANDEM-2) and transparent, “privacy-by-design” open architectures means international trust/learning can actually outlast a crisis.

Integration of “One Health” and environmental monitoring: Real operational drills are now integrating animal, environmental, and human surveillance—the hallmark of next-gen PPR.

Simulation as continuous improvement: Drills and data feeds loop into future grants and priority setting, not just in the U.S., but also in multi-country capacity-building (e.g., WHO Southeast Asia One Health collaborative ).

Lessons and Implications

The Tranquil Passport exercise is much more than a block-and-tackle drill. It’s a “live audit” of a decade’s worth of policy, technology, and system innovation, touching on every insight wrestled from COVID-19 and Ebola, and aiming to ensure we don’t repeat past mistakes:

Key themes:

Preparedness as everyday work: Drills, simulation, and scenario planning move from “annual box ticks” to real, continuous improvement/error-finding.

Open, collaborative science: Data flow, simulation platforms, and One Health collaborations are integrated into daily and crisis workflows.

Pragmatic innovation: Tools like the PBCU are no longer special-purpose but scalable, multi-jurisdictional assets, built for the demands of international response.

Equity and institutional memory: Budget cuts must not mean eroded capacity; new investment flows must prioritize both new tech and core health system basics.

Resilience beyond technology: Multi-sector engagement (across human, animal, and environmental health), open evaluation of after-action lessons, and public trust are as vital as any equipment.

The future:

Expect more, not fewer, full-scale exercises merging health, security, technology, and science, as the global community moves toward a seamless, all-hazards “preparedness culture.”

Preparedness, ultimately, is not static, it’s measured less by protocols on paper and more by drills like Tranquil Passport: practical, evidence-rich, globally connected.
