### **Introduction**

The equation of Jewish identity with Zionism has calcified to the point where questioning it reads as heresy. The standard story treats Jewish anti-Zionism as a recent development, something that surfaced after Israel occupied Palestinian territories in 1967 or grew out of contemporary progressive politics. The historical record points the other direction. For much of the early to mid-twentieth century, anti-Zionism was the default position among Jewish leftists, the mainstream view in Jewish working-class and radical political circles. This essay examines that history as it developed within the Communist Party USA and other leftist organizations from the Great Depression through the early Cold War, and considers what it means for current arguments about Israel/Palestine and the place of Jewish identity within global liberation struggles.

Benjamin Balthaser’s article, “When Anti-Zionism Was Jewish: Jewish Racial Subjectivity and the Anti-Imperialist Literary Left from the Great Depression to the Cold War,” documents this history. During the 1930s, roughly half of CPUSA membership was Jewish, and that contingent held an anti-Zionist position until 1947. The numbers undercut the claim that Zionism speaks for “the Jewish people” as a unified bloc. Balthaser frames this forgotten history as “a Jewish cultural resource for us to draw on,” one that shows “an important historical role in a diaspora has been solidarity with other oppressed people.”

This essay starts with the historical context of Jewish left-wing politics in the 1930s and 1940s, then moves to the theoretical foundations that tied Jewish identity to anti-imperialist and anti-colonial struggles. It examines how this history disappeared from mainstream memory, and how the Cold War reshaped Jewish political identity in the process. The diasporic framework that rejected territorial nationalism runs through both sections. The closing pages assess what this history means now, given Israel’s ongoing annexation of Palestinian territories and the growing use of apartheid comparisons, alongside new solidarity movements linking Palestinian liberation to other struggles for justice.

### **Jewish Left and anti-Zionism in the 1930s-1940s**

Jewish anti-Zionism in the 1930s and 1940s makes little sense unless you first look at who was doing the talking. The Jews making these arguments were, overwhelmingly, the children and grandchildren of Eastern European immigrants who’d arrived in the United States between the 1880s and the 1920s, fleeing pogroms and the grinding poverty of the Pale of Settlement. They landed in New York and a handful of other cities, packed into tenement neighborhoods, and went to work in garment shops and textile mills. Labor organizing and radical politics weren’t hobbies for these communities. They were the infrastructure of survival.

By the 1930s, Jewish workers made up a substantial chunk of the American industrial working class, concentrated in the garment and textile trades. That concentration gave radical organizers a ready-made base. Jewish immigrants showed up in socialist and anarchist movements in numbers well out of proportion to their share of the population, and the Communist Party USA, founded in 1919, pulled especially hard from this pool. During the 1930s, Jewish members accounted for roughly half the CPUSA’s total membership. You can’t grasp the political landscape of the period without that number sitting in front of you.

Zionism, to this audience, looked like a distraction dressed up as a solution. Robert Gessner put it bluntly in the New Masses: “In America, about one percent of America’s Jews are Zionist.” The socialist Left treated Zionists as nationalists and imperialist agents of the petite bourgeoisie, a small group that attracted more mockery than serious engagement. A Yiddish song circulating at the time called Zionists “little” and “foolish,” people who had lost all contact with “the workers’ reality.” The contempt was casual, almost offhand.

The anti-Zionist position wasn’t confined to one faction. The Jewish Daily Forward, the major Yiddish-language socialist paper, ran an anti-Zionist editorial line for decades. The Workmen’s Circle, the Arbeter Ring, a mutual aid and cultural organization with deep socialist roots, held that same line until the late 1940s. The CPUSA churned out pamphlets and study guides on the “Jewish Question” and Palestine, all of them articulating anti-Zionist arguments. Earl Browder, the party’s General Secretary, gave a major address called “Zionism” at the Hippodrome in 1936, framing the movement as a species of imperialism tied to British colonial interests.

The theoretical architecture behind all this belonged in large part to Alexander Bittelman, a Russian immigrant who became the CPUSA’s go-to thinker on Jewish identity. Bittelman edited the party’s theoretical journal, the Communist, and sat on the editorial board of Jewish Life, a communist-affiliated magazine. His mid-1940s study guide, “The Jewish Question,” laid out the argument in full. Global capitalism, Bittelman wrote, produced class immiseration and also produced “oppressed nations” like Puerto Rico and the Philippines, alongside “geographical colonialism” carried out through “economic and political pressure and intimidation.” He then turned the lens on the United States itself, describing a “system of national discrimination” that operated as “a peculiar system of oppression of peoples usually spoken of as minorities.”

Bittelman slotted Jewish identity into this broader anti-imperialist frame. Jews, he argued, did not constitute an “oppressed nation” in the way Puerto Ricans or African Americans did. Their “long-term material interests lay with the racially oppressed, not with the oppressors.” The move he was making was to pull Jewish identity out of the category of a separate national question demanding a territorial fix, and place it inside a global fight against racism and colonialism. For a Jewish leftist reading this in 1945, the implication was that your own liberation ran through the same channel as everyone else’s.

The Socialist Workers Party, which also carried substantial Jewish membership, held the same line. Their 1944 document, “Theses of Revolutionary Socialists on the Jewish Problem,” called Zionism a “sinister deviation from the true path” of Jewish liberation in Europe and part of “a larger reactionary project of white racism... aligned with elite interests.” The timing of that document matters. The SWP published it toward the end of the war, after the full scope of the Holocaust had started reaching a wider public. The standard narrative holds that the Holocaust made Zionism inevitable for American Jews. This document, written in the middle of that revelation, says otherwise.

Hannah Arendt, who’d fled Nazi Germany and was by then one of the most prominent Jewish intellectuals writing in English, made a parallel case. Her 1945 essay “Zionism Reconsidered,” published in Menorah Journal, described Zionism as “a project of elite brokerage between a small group of Zionists and the great powers of Europe and later the United States.” The movement, she argued, would “pass over” the “genuine national revolutionary movement which sprang from the Jewish masses.” Arendt saw an elitist project that had aligned itself with the most reactionary governments on the planet and called that alignment liberation.

Pull these threads together and the picture that emerges is one of consensus, not fringe opinion. Jewish anti-Zionism in the 1930s and 1940s was the default position among working-class Jewish radicals and the intellectuals who theorized alongside them. The argument rested on a specific framework linking Jewish identity to global anti-imperialist struggle, treating Zionism as a nationalism that served imperial interests and pulled energy away from class organizing. That position held across the Jewish Daily Forward, the Workmen’s Circle, the CPUSA, the SWP, and independent thinkers like Arendt. Balthaser calls it a “left-wing, working-class, anti-Zionist common sense,” and the phrase fits. The consistency across organizations and decades is what strikes you when you read the primary sources side by side. You keep finding the same argument, in the same language, from people who had no particular reason to coordinate.

### **Jewish Racial Subjectivity and anti-Imperialism**

Jewish leftists in the 1930s and 1940s built their anti-Zionism on a theoretical apparatus that Balthaser calls “Jewish racial subjectivity.” You can trace the architecture of it through their pamphlets, their fiction, their party newspapers. The claim underneath all of it: Jewishness belongs to the diaspora. It belongs to solidarity with other colonized and oppressed peoples. It has no business attaching itself to a flag or a border. This was a departure from the communal identity most American Jews inherited, and it ran against the Zionist narrative that was consolidating at the same time. Balthaser’s term captures something the primary sources make plain. These writers and organizers treated Jewish identity as a position within a global anti-imperialist struggle, and they built their politics outward from that premise.

Jewish liminality sat at the center of the whole edifice. Jews in America occupied an ambiguous slot in the racial hierarchy. They were white enough to pass in most institutions, and they were marked enough to face quotas, violence, and exclusion. Balthaser argues that Jewish leftists treated this in-between status as a political resource. They could cross racial lines in ways that fully enfranchised white Americans had no incentive to cross them. The quote from Balthaser gets the mechanism right: Jewish leftists articulated “a Jewish liminality, between whiteness and blackness, that performed the exact opposite [of assimilation]: solidarity with other oppressed groups.” The liminal position did the work. Assimilation would have closed it off.

Mike Gold made this argument in fiction as well as in party journalism. His novel “Jews Without Money” tied Jewish identity to class struggle and to the lives of other exploited workers, and his shorter pieces kept returning to the same knot. In 1948, during the first Arab-Israeli War, Gold published a short story in Jewish Life about a Jewish fur trader and his son who “dwelt and traded” among Indigenous peoples. The story places Jewishness inside a network of cross-racial contact and mutual dependence. Territory drops out of the picture. The trader’s identity comes from whom he lives alongside, not from a plot of land he owns.

The CPUSA line on Palestine after World War II, printed in Jewish Life, called for Jews and Arabs to build multi-ethnic coalitions, push the British out, and share a bi-cultural, bi-national state. Historians have documented how hard that would have been to pull off on the ground, given how segregated Jewish settlement already was by the late 1940s. For the American Jewish activists reading Jewish Life in Brooklyn or the Lower East Side, though, the proposal functioned as what Balthaser calls “a utopian way for the Jewish Left to imagine Jews in Palestine within an anti-colonial framework.” The bi-national state gave them a way to keep Jewish presence in Palestine legible inside an anti-colonial politics. The Zionist narrative offered a Jewish state as the sole answer to the “Jewish Question.” The CPUSA line refused that framing and folded Jewish liberation into a broader anti-colonial struggle instead.

Solidarity, as these writers used the term, carried a built-in tension that Balthaser names plainly. He writes that “the concept of solidarity has always embedded in it a potential for bad universality, a tendency to flatten differences among disparate groups, yet such calls for solidarity also create the framework for dominant groups to break with hegemonic forms of identity construction and to question legitimated forms of violence, privilege, and exclusion.” The Jewish leftists in Balthaser’s archive knew the risk. A solidarity that sanded down every difference between a Black sharecropper in Mississippi and a Jewish garment worker in New York would produce a politics too thin to organize anyone. The solidarity they wanted recognized shared subjection to imperialism and racism while keeping the specific histories of each group visible.

Howard Fast’s 1947 story “Epitaph for Sidney,” also published in Jewish Life, dramatizes this version of solidarity through a single life. Sidney Greenspan fights fascism in Spain. He fights Jim Crow in the American South. He dies in Italy. The closing passage has his friends gathering to write an elegy, and the narrator describes Sidney’s relationship to place: “They buried Sidney Greenspan in Italian soil, good soil, and the soil of Spain is good, too, and the soil of America, and the soil of the Soviet Union, and of China, and if he had his choice, I don’t think there is any place he wouldn’t have been at home, fully and completely at home.”

Balthaser reads Sidney as a “deterritorialized subject, someone who could be ‘at home’ anywhere,” a figure rooted in the earth and connected to peoples across the globe at the same time. The detail that matters most, for Balthaser’s argument, is the absence in the passage. Sidney’s diaspora “never mentions Palestine.” His Jewishness is “a nonplaced defined by gestures of solidarity.” The story’s final lines drive the point home: “To the memory of Sidney Greenspan, anti-fascist, who fell in the people’s struggle, from his comrades.” Sidney belongs to a global liberation struggle. He belongs to no specific territory.

The religious register in Fast’s story deserves attention. Balthaser notes that Sidney’s socialist newspapers, “with heavy black lines to bind them,” recall tefillin. Sidney’s “secular socialist identity is a kind of observant Judaism.” The parallel pulls Jewish ritual into the frame of anti-Zionist politics. Judith Butler’s intellectual history of anti-Zionism makes a related move. She writes that “Jewish values of cohabitation with the non-Jew... are part of the very ethical substance of diasporic Jewishness.” For the writers Balthaser studies, anti-Zionism expressed a particular reading of Jewish ethics. The obligation to the stranger, the tradition of cohabitation, the diasporic condition as a moral resource rather than a wound to be healed by a state. These commitments pointed away from territorial sovereignty and toward the kind of solidarity Sidney Greenspan embodied.

The framework had teeth in practical politics. Jewish leftists who held these views treated Zionism as incompatible with their anti-imperialist commitments, and they said so in print. Gessner, writing in New Masses under the title “Brown Shirts in Zion,” accused Zionists of wishing to “dispose of the Arabs as so many Indians.” The comparison to American settler colonialism was deliberate. Gessner placed Zionist settlement in the same category as the dispossession of Indigenous peoples in the United States. Zionism, in this reading, replicated the structures of oppression that Jewish leftists were fighting in every other context. You could not oppose American colonialism at home and endorse its mirror image in Palestine.

Balthaser’s archive shows that Jewish anti-Zionism in this period was a worked-out reimagining of Jewish identity inside a global anti-imperialist frame. It offered a version of Jewishness rooted in diaspora and in solidarity with other colonized peoples. That version challenged the Zionist claim that Jewish identity required territorial sovereignty. It also challenged the assimilationist claim that Jewish identity required absorption into American whiteness. Both of those narratives treated Jewishness as a problem with a fixed solution. The Jewish leftists Balthaser studies treated it as a position from which to act, and they acted accordingly, in their fiction, their party organs, and their organizing, for roughly two decades before the political ground shifted under them.

### **The Erasure of Jewish anti-Zionist History**

Jewish anti-Zionism in the 1930s and 1940s was theoretically sophisticated and widespread, and mainstream narratives of Jewish identity have buried it. Balthaser calls this burial “a larger ideological and material project that included an incomplete but nonetheless wide incorporation of immigrant Jewish life into dominant American ideas of race, politics, and identity.” You can trace how the equation of Jewishness with Zionism became normalized only if you first understand how the alternative got scrubbed from the record.

Karen Brodkin documented the racial repositioning in “How Jews Became White Folks and What That Says about Race in America.” Eastern European Jewish immigrants started out racially ambiguous in the American hierarchy, coded as non-white or somewhere in between. After World War II, state policies and economic openings pulled Jews into whiteness. Balthaser calls the whole process “a bloody matrix of assimilation and whiteness that emerged out of the Cold War suburbanization of the 1950s.” The shift required active political choices from Jewish communities, and those choices came with strings attached.

Balthaser puts the bargain plainly: “Israel was part of that devil’s bargain. Yes, you can become real Americans: You can go to good U.S. universities, you can join the suburbs, enter into the mainstream of American life, as long as you do this one little thing for us, which is back the American Empire.” Support for Israel functioned as the price of admission into American whiteness and the imperial project that whiteness carried with it. Jewish identity got tethered to a foreign policy position, and the tether was conditional.

McCarthy-era repression did the mechanical work of silencing the organized left that had housed Jewish anti-Zionism. The Communist Party USA served as the primary organizational home for that politics, and government persecution gutted it. Then the Soviet Union endorsed Partition in 1947, and Balthaser notes that this “caused many Jewish leftists to shift their position on Zionism,” rupturing what had been a unified anti-Zionist stance on the Jewish left. You had repression from outside and a split from inside, arriving at roughly the same moment.

Bernard Lazare identified the historiographical problem early: “Jewish history has been written by bourgeois historians for a bourgeois readership, radical Jews, especially working-class radical Jews, rarely figure much.” Bourgeois Jewish historians produced a sanitized record emphasizing upward mobility and integration. Working-class radical politics got left out of the story, and once they were out, the story read as though they had never been in it.

The Cold War finished the job by collapsing all political questions into a binary. You supported Israel, or you supported Soviet interests in the Middle East. Jewish anti-Zionists who opposed both American and Soviet imperial aims had no slot in that framework, and the framework associated their position with disloyalty. The binary did the silencing that McCarthyism had started.

Institutions shifted alongside the political climate. The Workmen’s Circle and the Jewish Daily Forward, both of which had maintained anti-Zionist positions, gradually moved toward supporting Israel. New organizations formed around defending the state, and they treated criticism of Israeli policy as antisemitism. The Anti-Defamation League, which bills itself as a civil rights organization, has in recent decades become “a shield to protect Israel from accountability and defend the state’s racist policies,” with “a documented history of surveilling Black, Palestinian, and other progressive organizers and supporting racist policing.”

The understanding of Jewish identity itself got rewritten in this period. The diasporic, solidarity-based Jewishness that characterized the Jewish left gave way to a Zionist framework placing the State of Israel at the center of Jewish identity and positioning it as the answer to antisemitism. By the late twentieth century, opposing Zionism read as a betrayal of Jewish identity. The earlier tradition, where opposing Zionism was a legitimate expression of that same identity, had disappeared from common knowledge.

This erasure distorts contemporary political discourse in specific ways. It makes Jewish opposition to Zionism look like a recent development, a novelty, rather than a tradition with decades of intellectual and organizational history behind it. It severs the visible connection between Jewish identity and global anti-imperialist struggle. And it flattens the range of Jewish political thought down to a single acceptable position, so that anyone outside that position has to explain themselves rather than simply speak.

Israel’s ongoing annexation of Palestinian territories and the growing currency of apartheid comparisons have forced a reexamination of the relationship between Jewish identity and Zionism. The debates happening now look new to people who encountered only the post-erasure version of Jewish political history. They are continuations of tensions that Jewish communities argued through in the 1930s and 1940s, before the record got rewritten and the earlier arguments got filed away as though they had never happened.

### **The Cold War Turning Point and the Transformation of Jewish Politics**

After 1945, Jewish anti-Zionism split apart. The left had opposed Zionism as a bloc for decades, and then the Cold War pried that bloc open along seams that had been there all along, waiting for pressure. The fracture followed specific contingencies, the kind historians can actually trace through party minutes and pamphlet runs, and it reshaped what “Jewish political identity” meant for the people living through it.

The Soviet Union voted for the UN Partition Plan for Palestine in 1947, and that vote broke the international communist movement’s anti-Zionist line overnight. The Communist Party USA had held an anti-Zionist position for decades. Moscow’s reversal put Jewish communists in the party in a bind they couldn’t talk their way out of. Balthaser puts it plainly: “The Soviet Union’s endorsement of Partition in 1947 caused many Jewish leftists to shift their position on Zionism.” You had people whose entire political life ran through the party, and the party just told them the thing they’d opposed for twenty years was now the correct line.

Stalin backed Partition for geopolitical reasons. He wanted to pry British influence out of the Middle East and maybe install a socialist ally in the region. The calculation had nothing to do with a revised reading of Zionist ideology. Communist internationalism and Zionist nationalism still contradicted each other in every way that mattered, and Jewish communists absorbed that contradiction directly, in local party cells, in arguments that went nowhere.

Jewish leftists felt the squeeze in their own organizing lives. The Soviet endorsement of Partition read, to many of them, as a betrayal of the anti-imperialist principles that had given their anti-Zionism its spine. Balthaser writes that this moment “laid bare the tension between internationalist commitments and national(ist) projects” that the earlier period of unified opposition had kept buried under shared slogans and shared enemies.

The 1960s brought new organizations into the mix and made the politics harder to map. Students for a Democratic Society, which had a “very sizeable Jewish membership,” formally backed anti-Zionism. The Socialist Workers Party did the same. Both groups built alliances with the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, which took an official anti-Zionist position in the late 1960s. Balthaser describes the result as “a global revolutionary framework in which Palestinian liberation was an articulated part,” a framework that linked the Palestine Liberation Organization and the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine to revolutionary movements across three continents.

This 1960s anti-Zionism looked different from the working-class anti-Zionism of the 1930s and 1940s. The Jewish left now drew from the children of earlier immigrants alongside newer waves of Jewish activists. Its anti-Zionism ran through Third World liberation movements and operated outside the Communist Party’s organizational machinery, which meant it answered to different constituencies and different internal debates.

The Holocaust’s full scale entered public consciousness in the immediate postwar years, and that knowledge carried an emotional weight that made the case for a Jewish state feel, to many Jews, like a moral obligation rather than a political preference. Balthaser identifies the narrative directly: “Perhaps the single most pervasive narrative about Zionism, even among scholars and writers who acknowledge its marginal status before the war, is that the Holocaust changed Jewish opinion and convinced Jews of its necessity.”

Balthaser pushes back on that narrative by pointing to the Socialist Workers Party and to Hannah Arendt, both of whom held anti-Zionist positions after the Holocaust became widely understood. The link between genocide and statehood was assembled through specific political work, through organizing and argument and institutional pressure, rather than arriving as some automatic psychological response to atrocity.

The Cold War made the whole terrain harder to occupy. Washington framed every Middle East question as a loyalty test. Supporting Israel meant supporting American interests. Criticizing Israel meant handing Moscow a talking point. Jewish anti-Zionists who tried to hold an anti-imperialist position found their arguments read as disloyalty, and that reading stuck in ways that outlasted the Cold War itself.

American racial politics pulled in the same direction. Jewish Americans gained access to whiteness in the postwar decades, through suburbanization, through expanded economic opportunity, through the slow grinding work of being accepted into institutions that had barred them a generation earlier. As that incorporation proceeded, political perspectives tilted toward alignment with American imperial interests. The shift was gradual and uneven, but it was measurable in voting patterns, in organizational memberships, in which causes got funded and which got ignored.

The diasporic, solidarity-based understanding of Jewishness that had anchored the Jewish left gave way to a Zionist framework that placed the State of Israel at the center of Jewish identity. By the late twentieth century, opposing Zionism read, to most American Jews, as a betrayal of Jewishness. The earlier tradition, the one that treated Jewish identity as compatible with anti-nationalism, had been marginalized so thoroughly that younger Jews encountering it for the first time often assumed it was a recent invention.

These postwar fractures explain why current debates over Israel/Palestine feel both urgent and repetitive. The tensions running through Jewish political thought today descend directly from the splits of the 1940s and 1960s. The recent resurgence of Jewish anti-Zionism draws on those earlier traditions while responding to conditions those traditions never faced: Israel’s ongoing annexation of Palestinian territories, the apartheid comparisons gaining traction in human rights reporting, and a generation of Jewish activists who grew up after the Cold War binary lost its grip.

### **Beyond Territorial Nationalism**

The Jewish anti-Zionist tradition does something specific with the question of identity: it pulls Jewishness away from the soil and plants it in the relationship between people. Balthaser calls this “a nonplaced defined by gestures of solidarity,” and the phrase does real work. Jewishness, in this reading, becomes an ethical commitment you practice alongside other oppressed groups.

Most of Jewish history happened outside Palestine/Israel. That’s the plain demographic fact, and the diasporic framework takes it as a starting point rather than a wound. Jewish communities in Baghdad, Kraków, Cairo, and Brooklyn built religious and cultural traditions that had no need for a flag or a border checkpoint. The diasporic reading treats that scattered existence as the thing that generated ethical insight in the first place, the condition that made cohabitation a practiced skill rather than an abstract ideal.

Cohabitation sits at the center of this. Jewish survival depended on living alongside non-Jewish communities, negotiating shared space, building working relationships across difference. Judith Butler puts it plainly in her intellectual history of anti-Zionism: “Jewish values of cohabitation with the non-Jew... are part of the very ethical substance of diasporic Jewishness.” You can see the friction with the Zionist project immediately. Establishing Jewish sovereignty over territory where another people already lives requires you to set cohabitation aside, to make the relationship conditional on one group’s political dominance.

The nation-state model is a recent invention, a few centuries old at most, and Jewish anti-Zionists noticed early that it tended to produce exclusion and violence alongside whatever liberation it promised. Jewish identity, in their account, survived and expressed itself through cultural practice, religious observance, ethical argument. None of that required a passport. The state was one option among many, and a costly one.

Howard Fast’s “Epitaph for Sidney” gives this a face. Sidney Greenspan is “at home” in the US, the Soviet Union, China, Italy. His belonging comes from showing up in liberation struggles wherever they happen. The territory is incidental. The commitment is the address. Fast writes a character whose identity is legible through participation in global struggle, and the story treats that as whole, as sufficient. You don’t sense a gap where a homeland should be.

The religious thread runs through this too, and it complicates any easy secular/sacred split. Fast lingers on tefillin in the story, those “heavy black lines to bind them,” and the binding becomes a figure for something larger. Sidney’s “secular socialist identity is a kind of observant Judaism.” The solidarity practice and the religious practice share a grammar. Jewish tradition carries resources for thinking about justice and community that have nothing to do with a plot of land, and the anti-Zionist religious thinkers drew on those resources directly.

Positioning Jewishness as a diasporic identity defined through solidarity also cuts against racialization from two directions at once. The Zionist narrative ties Jewish identity to racial belonging in a specific territory. The antisemitic narrative casts Jews as permanently alien, disloyal to whatever country houses them. The diasporic framework refuses both. Jewishness is a practice of ethical commitment, portable, legible in any context where people organize against oppression.

For the Israel/Palestine debate, this reframing does concrete work. It separates Jewish identity from the requirement of supporting a Jewish state. It makes Jewishness compatible with Palestinian self-determination without any sense of betrayal or loss. The ethical commitments that define diasporic Jewishness, justice and cohabitation, point toward Palestinian freedom rather than away from it.

The current moment has sharpened the stakes. Israel’s ongoing annexation of Palestinian territories and the growing use of the apartheid framework have pushed more Jews to ask whether their identity requires them to defend those policies. The diasporic reading gives them a way to say no. You can reject the violence of settler colonialism and still be fully, recognizably Jewish. You can also refuse the antisemitic move that tries to exclude Jews from broader liberation work because of what a state does in their name.

The implications stretch past the Jewish case. The diasporic framework questions the assumption that territorial sovereignty is the only answer available to an oppressed group. Other models exist. Identity defined through ethical commitment, through practiced solidarity across borders, offers a path that doesn’t require drawing a new line on a map and calling it freedom.

For Jews in the United States, the framework addresses a specific tension. You belong to an “historically persecuted ethno-cultural diasporic minority” and you also participate in American systems of power and privilege. Both are true at once. Balthaser names the work this requires: “For Jews in the United States who are trying to think about their relationship, not only to Palestine, but also their own place in the world as an historically persecuted ethno-cultural diasporic minority, we have to think of whose side we are on, and which global forces we want to align with.”

This tradition gives contemporary Jewish politics a foundation that reaches back through a century of anti-Zionist thought while staying responsive to conditions on the ground right now. The alliances it builds with other oppressed groups rest on a shared understanding of identity as ethical practice.

### **Annexation, Apartheid, and Solidarity Movements**

Israel’s plan to annex large swaths of the West Bank, delayed for now but still on the table, has “laid bare the violence of the Zionist project of establishing Jewish rule over a Palestinian population.” Apartheid comparisons that used to circulate in fringe pamphlets now show up in mainstream op-eds. And that collision, between a state accelerating its land grabs and a growing chorus calling the project what it looks like, has dragged the old question of Jewish anti-Zionism back into rooms where people would prefer it stayed settled.

Balthaser calls this “an important moment to connect with the history of Jewish anti-Zionism,” and the timing is hard to argue with. Peter Beinart, a liberal Zionist who spent years defending the two-state framework, now says that framework is dead and that one state with equal rights is the only path left. A figure like Beinart conceding that point would have been unthinkable in mainstream Zionist discourse a decade ago. The old arguments Jewish anti-Zionists made in the 1930s and 1940s are landing with people who never would have entertained them before. Balthaser puts the stakes in plain terms: “If we do not want to side with the executioners of the far-right, with colonialism and with racism, there is a Jewish cultural resource for us to draw on, a political resource to draw on. This history of the anti-Zionist Jewish left demonstrates that an important historical role in a diaspora has been solidarity with other oppressed people.”

You can see this playing out in the organizations forming right now. Jewish Voice for Peace and the Jewish Solidarity Caucus of the Democratic Socialists of America, groups made up of Jews and non-Jews questioning the U.S. role in propping up Zionism, are pulling arguments straight from the Jewish left of the 1930s and 1940s. Palestinian liberation sits inside a broader anti-imperialist, anti-racist struggle in their framing, the same way it did for the Bundists and the Communist Jewish organizers ninety years ago.

The whiteness question is where this history gets uncomfortable for a lot of American Jews. Balthaser doesn’t soften it: “It’s a bloody matrix of assimilation and whiteness that emerged out of the Cold War sub-urbanization of the 1950s. Israel was part of that devil’s bargain. Yes, you can become real Americans: You can go to good U.S. universities, you can join the suburbs, enter into the mainstream of American life, as long as you do this one little thing for us, which is back the American Empire.” That bargain, access to suburbs and universities and mainstream acceptance in exchange for loyalty to a military project, held for decades because nobody in power had a reason to challenge it.

Israel’s growing embrace of apartheid policies, and the documented overlap between Israeli military tactics and American policing practices, have cracked that arrangement open. The recognition that backing Israel functioned as the price of admission into American whiteness means a Jewish political identity built on solidarity with other oppressed groups, rather than on complicity with empire, is no longer a fringe position. It’s a conversation happening in DSA chapters and on college campuses and in synagogues that would have shut it down five years ago.

The global revolutionary framework that shaped historical Jewish anti-Zionism still maps onto the present. Balthaser traces the lineage: “Students for a Democratic Society, which also had a very sizeable Jewish membership, formally backed anti-Zionism in the 1960s, along with the Socialist Workers Party, and formed alliances with the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, which had also taken an official anti-Zionist position in the late 1960s. You could think about a global revolutionary framework in which Palestinian liberation was an articulated part, you could think about the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine and the Palestine Liberation Organization as part of the fabric of global revolutionary movements.” SDS, the SWP, SNCC, the PFLP, the PLO. These groups saw Palestinian liberation as one thread in a larger fabric of anti-colonial struggle, and that framing is back.

The Boycott, Divestment, and Sanctions movement draws the same connections today, linking Palestinian liberation to fights against racism and colonialism across borders. Balthaser describes the resulting alignment as “a global coalition” that is “necessary to help achieve” Palestinian liberation. The architecture looks different from the Popular Front era, but the underlying logic, that you can’t free one group while ignoring the systems that oppress others, hasn’t changed.

The point of recovering this history is the resource it offers. Balthaser is clear that nobody is trying to rebuild the Communist Party of the 1930s: “So I look at this not as saying, ‘We’re not going to reproduce the Communist Party of the 1930s and 1940s.’ We’re saying, ‘We’ll produce something new, but the past can be a cultural resource that we can use today.’” A cultural and political inheritance, available to anyone building something that fits the conditions they actually face.

Israel’s annexation plans and the apartheid comparisons they’ve triggered have blown open a question that mainstream Jewish politics tried to close decades ago. The anti-Zionist Jewish left of the 1930s and 1940s left behind a body of argument, a set of organizational models, and a tradition of cross-racial solidarity that groups like JVP and the DSA Jewish Solidarity Caucus are picking back up.

### **Conclusion**

Jewish anti-Zionism in the 1930s and 1940s was the mainstream position among working-class and radical Jewish intellectuals, and it carried a worked-out theoretical framework that placed Jewish identity inside a global anti-imperialist struggle. The labor press and radical pamphlets of that period lay this framework out in considerable detail. You find it argued page after page in publications that circulated through garment districts and union halls, making the case that Jewishness meant something specific and political outside the nation-state. The position had intellectual architecture behind it. It was the default, the thing you’d encounter before you’d encounter the alternative.

The assumption that Jewish identity requires Zionism is a contingent historical product, and the processes that produced it are identifiable. The Cold War-era incorporation of Jews into American whiteness did most of the work, and the erasure of working-class Jewish perspectives from public memory sealed it. Jewish opposition to Zionism stretches back decades before anyone now calling themselves “anti-Zionist” was born. The equation of Jewishness with support for Israel took shape through those specific pressures. It was never the only option on the table, and the people who’d once filled rooms arguing the other side got written out of the record.

The diasporic framework that historical Jewish anti-Zionists developed positioned Jewishness as an ethical commitment to solidarity with other oppressed groups. Jewish identity persisted without a state, expressed through religious and cultural practice that crossed national boundaries. A Jew in Warsaw or New York or Buenos Aires could maintain and articulate that identity through community, through text, through the obligations you owed to people suffering under the same systems you suffered under. This framework is compatible with Palestinian self-determination. It requires no territorial claim to function.

Jewish identity has always been contested. Different communities and political movements reimagined it in response to changing conditions, and the version that won out in the mid-twentieth century was one version among several that had been in active competition for decades.

The forgotten history of Jewish anti-Zionism corrects the standard account of what Jewish identity is and has been. It also points toward a Jewish politics defined by ethical commitments to justice and solidarity with other peoples fighting for liberation, a politics that doesn’t route through territorial claims or national loyalty to function. That vision existed. People built institutions around it. The fact that most readers have never encountered it says something about who controlled the archive after 1945, and the recovery of that history gives contemporary Jewish political thought a set of options it’s been told don’t exist.
