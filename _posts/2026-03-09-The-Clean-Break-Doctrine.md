### **Introduction**

In the summer of 1996, a six-page document moved through political circles in Jerusalem. It contained a set of recommendations that eventually changed the trajectory of the Middle East. The authors titled their strategy A Clean Break. They specifically told Benjamin Netanyahu, who was just starting his first term as Israel’s Prime Minister, to walk away from peace talks. Their plan focused on ousting Saddam Hussein and using proxy groups to destabilize Syria. The influence of this memo didn’t stop in Israel. Seven of the men behind these ideas later took over high-ranking roles in the George W. Bush administration. Once in power, they had the authority to view classified files and draft military strategies. They even set up their own intelligence units to get around the standard oversight of the CIA. By the time the United States invaded Iraq in 2003, these officials were following the core advice of their own 1996 paper. This exposé follows the paper trail from that original study group to the actual policy shifts in Washington. It looks at the goals these men reached, the details they kept hidden, and the fallout of turning a theoretical think tank memo into a literal blueprint for war.

### **The Study Group That Became a Government**

Richard Perle sat at the head of the table when the Institute for Advanced Strategic and Political Studies gathered its study group. They were there to map out a new Israeli strategy for the year 2000. This Jerusalem think tank basically commissioned the project as a guidebook for Benjamin Netanyahu’s incoming Likud government. Perle had the kind of resume that made people in Washington pay attention. He had already run Global Strategic Affairs as Assistant Secretary of Defense under Reagan. By the time this group met, his name carried massive weight in the neoconservative circles that had been gaining steam since the seventies.

The room was filled with people who weren’t just academics. Douglas Feith was there, coming from his law firm Feith and Zell, which handled defense contracts. David Wurmser was a research fellow at the institute, working alongside his wife Meyrav. She had a doctorate from George Washington University and had written her thesis on Revisionist Zionism. You also had James Colbert from the Jewish Institute for National Security Affairs and Charles Fairbanks from Johns Hopkins. Robert Loewenberg, the institute president, and Jonathan Torop from the Washington Institute for Near East Policy rounded out the list.

It is easy to skip past the names in the participants section at the back of the document. The report treats them like an afterthought. But that layout hides the reality of where these people ended up. If you look at government organizational charts over the next couple of decades, these same names keep popping up in the most powerful offices in America.

Perle eventually chaired the Defense Policy Board Advisory Committee, a spot that gave him a direct line to the top of the Pentagon. Feith became the Under Secretary of Defense for Policy. That made him the third-highest person in the entire Department of Defense, putting him in charge of actual strategic planning and policy. David Wurmser moved right into the White House as a Middle East Adviser for Vice President Dick Cheney.

Paul Wolfowitz helped build the intellectual bones of the Clean Break report and later became the Deputy Secretary of Defense, second only to Donald Rumsfeld. Then there was Lewis Scooter Libby. He was tied to this same crowd through the Project for the New American Century and ended up as Cheney’s Chief of Staff. These weren’t just analysts writing a paper. They were the future architects of American foreign policy.

The personnel pipeline worked with startling efficiency. You can see it in how a policy paper written for an Israeli prime minister in 1996 suddenly reappeared within the US national security apparatus by 2001. This happened by design. The Project for the New American Century, which William Kristol and Robert Kagan started in 1997, acted as the central hub for this crowd. When PNAC released its Rebuilding America’s Defenses report in 2000, it pushed for massive defense spending and a much more aggressive American presence globally. The wording was a mirror image of the Clean Break recommendations.

These two documents shared the same authors. Both papers promoted regime change as a standard tool for foreign policy. They both walked away from the idea of containment, choosing proactive military intervention instead.

In September 2002, Brian Whitaker wrote in the Guardian about how several Clean Break authors had secured powerful roles in Washington. He noticed something strange about this shift. Usually, foreign policy advisors drift in and out of different administrations with varying levels of pull. This group was different. They stayed tight across multiple presidencies. They wrote together and they testified together. They spent years hammering home the exact same policies. By the time George W. Bush took office in January 2001, this network held the specific offices needed to turn their 1996 ideas into reality. The invasion of Iraq followed two years later. Looking at that timeline, it looks like a long-term plan rather than a sudden response to events.

### **The Intellectual Architecture of Regime Change**

The Clean Break paper starts by calling out what it sees as a long-running failure. For seventy years, Labor Zionism ran the show, but the authors argue this just left Israel with a trapped, sluggish economy. They had no patience for the “New Middle East” branding used during the peace process. In their eyes, that whole approach didn’t just fail; it actually stripped away national legitimacy and left the country stuck in a strategic rut.

The tone here is pretty clear. The writers look at diplomatic compromise and see nothing but weakness. To them, giving up land is the same thing as surrendering. They hated the Oslo Accords and everything that deal stood for. One of their biggest gripes was how Israel used the United States. They felt the government was basically hiding behind American coattails to push through policies that people at home didn’t even want. Instead of seeing the U.S. as a helpful partner, they viewed this dynamic as a major drag on Israel’s own power.

To fix this, they laid out a four-part plan.

The first step was to walk away from Oslo entirely. They wanted to stop treating the PLO as a group that could actually represent Palestinians. Next, they aimed to shift how the neighborhood worked. This meant getting closer to Turkey and Jordan while putting the squeeze on Syria. The third part was about picking fights. They pushed for a more aggressive, face-to-face confrontation with countries like Iraq and Iran. Finally, they wanted to change how Israel handled its own safety. The goal was to stop relying on diplomats and start leaning much harder on raw military strength and self-reliance. The document gets into the weeds on exactly how to pull each of these off.

The Iraq section is particularly striking. The report basically argues that Israel can remodel its entire neighborhood by weakening or rolling back Syria. Central to this plan is the removal of Saddam Hussein. It is not framed as a “what if” or a backup plan. The authors treat toppling a head of state as a direct, actionable strategic goal. They seem to operate on the belief that an Iraq without Baathist control would naturally lean toward peace and normal ties with Israel. It treats regime change like a simple toggle switch for regional stability.

The logic regarding Syria follows that same track. The document points to the Damascus-Tehran alliance as a massive weak spot in the regional power balance. It pushes for Israel to actively break that link using pressure and destabilization. The authors want Israel to take the initiative on the northern borders by treating Hezbollah, Syria, and Iran as the main aggressors in Lebanon.

They suggest specific tactics to make this happen. One idea involves hitting Syria’s drug money and counterfeiting operations located in Lebanon. The goal is to set a precedent that Syrian territory is fair game for retaliatory strikes. If causing trouble for Syria within Lebanon doesn’t work, the plan calls for hitting targets inside Syria itself. It is a ladder of escalation designed to hammer the Assad regime without committing to a full-scale invasion.

The Palestinian policy recommendations in the document toss out the land for peace formula entirely. It argues that being morally vague about building a Jewish state versus trading land won’t actually bring peace. The plan says Israel needs to keep the right of hot pursuit for self-defense into all Palestinian areas. It also suggests finding ways to break Arafat’s total control over Palestinian society. This way of thinking set the stage for what happened later during the Second Intifada. You can see the roots of the targeted assassinations of Hamas and Fatah leaders here. Regular military raids into Palestinian cities and the building of the separation barrier both fit into this security-first mindset. Clean Break gave these moves an intellectual backbone by describing them as essential responses to a permanently hostile force instead of just temporary fixes while waiting for a deal.

The strategy for handling the United States is just as deliberate. The text claims Israel basically invited the U.S. to meddle in its internal and foreign affairs for two specific reasons. One was to help push through land concessions that the Israeli public wouldn’t accept on their own. The other was to dangle American money, weapons, and political favors to get Arab leaders to the table. This approach meant sending American tax dollars to aggressive, repressive regimes. It was an expensive and risky move for both countries. The alternative proposed here is all about self-reliance and a more mature partnership. It suggests Israel should actually stop taking the kind of aid that gets in the way of economic reform. Doing this would cut down on how much leverage Washington has over Israeli decisions while still keeping the door open for military cooperation on things like missile defense.

Patrick Buchanan noted back in 2003 that the Clean Break plan pushed Israel to embrace the principle of preemption. That description really hits on the core shift in thinking found in the document. Preemption is a specific beast. It gives the green light for military action before a threat even shows up instead of waiting to react to an actual attack. This idea turned into a pillar of the Bush Doctrine in the 2002 National Security Strategy. When you look at the 1996 recommendations in Clean Break and compare them to the 2002 doctrine, it looks less like a coincidence and more like a straight line of thought.

Writing for the New York Times in August 2003, Ian Buruma pointed out that Douglas Feith and Richard Perle were the ones advising Netanyahu to walk away from the Oslo Accords in 1996. Their argument was pretty bold. They claimed Israel would be safer if the regimes in neighboring countries were replaced. Buruma wrote this after the invasion of Iraq was already underway. Because of that timing, he was able to look back and see the clear link between a years-old memo and the reality of modern policy.

The Office of Special Plans and Intelligence Manipulation
Douglas Feith ran the Office of Special Plans at the Pentagon while the US was moving toward war with Iraq. This unit did not follow the usual intelligence paths. Instead, the OSP built its own assessments to back the administration’s goal of removing Saddam Hussein from power. Many observers argued the office ignored inconvenient data and focused only on worst-case scenarios.

The Pentagon Inspector General eventually looked into these claims. The resulting report detailed some questionable habits. It showed that OSP officials created and shared their own versions of the relationship between Iraq and Al Qaeda. They did the same with reports on weapons of mass destruction. These internal OSP findings frequently went against what the CIA and other agencies were saying at the time.

The link between the OSP and the authors of the Clean Break memo is significant. It shows how specific policy goals can steer the way intelligence is gathered and used. Feith helped write that 1996 memo which called for Hussein’s ouster. A few years later, he was in charge of the very unit producing the evidence needed to make that happen. It looks like a closed loop where the preferred answer dictates which facts get picked up.

In September 2004, the Washington Post noted that FBI investigators questioned David Wurmser, Feith, Harold Rhode, and Paul Wolfowitz. The inquiry involved the potential leak of classified secrets to Ahmad Chalabi and the American Israel Public Affairs Committee. Chalabi headed the Iraqi National Congress. This exile group supplied a large portion of the intel used to make the case for the invasion. Later reviews proved that much of that information was either made up or blown out of proportion.

The pressure didn’t stop at the Office of Special Plans. Dick Cheney’s team practically lived at the CIA, constantly pushing for a second look at raw data. They weren’t looking for a broad view. They wanted one specific thing: a link between Iraq and Al Qaeda. Standard analysis hadn’t found it, so the Vice President’s office kept digging until they got the answers they needed.

Once those links were “found,” the administration ran with them. Officials talked about the Iraq-Al Qaeda connection in almost every public speech to build the case for war. The 9/11 Commission eventually cleared the air, stating there was no real evidence of the two groups working together. But the truth came out too late. The invasion was already a reality, and you can’t exactly un-ring that bell.

You can see the same logic in the Clean Break report. It changed how people thought about threats. Instead of seeing regional rivals as governments that might want to negotiate, the document labeled them as enemies obsessed with destroying Israel. It stripped away the idea of “rational actors” and replaced it with the idea of existential threats.

This shift made preemptive strikes feel like the only option. If you believe your enemy is on a suicide mission, you stop trying to deter them and start trying to eliminate them. Usually, intelligence officers separate what a country can do from what it wants to do. Clean Break ignored that boundary. It treated a hostile attitude as a good enough reason for war, no matter what the actual military capabilities looked like.

Things started falling apart pretty quickly once the invasion actually happened. Not a single weapon of mass destruction turned up. It turned out the intelligence community built their entire case for chemical and biological weapons on shaky, incomplete data that just didn’t hold water. The OSP made things worse by shouting those specific reports from the rooftops while keeping quiet about any evidence that said otherwise.

When the dust settled, the distance between what was promised and what was actually on the ground shredded American credibility. People everywhere started asking if the intel was twisted on purpose to fit a plan that was already decided. Even with all that, the Clean Break authors never really owned up to it. They just kept saying that getting rid of Saddam Hussein was the right move no matter what the weapons inspectors found.

### **Iraq as Laboratory for State Fragmentation**

The 2003 invasion of Iraq turned the core ideas of the Clean Break memo into a literal roadmap. Getting rid of Saddam Hussein was a major strategic goal for Israel in that document, and the Bush administration essentially adopted it as a primary mission. They just wrapped it in the language of the War on Terror.

The actual military push mirrored the memo’s logic. It relied on massive force to make the regime collapse fast. After the fighting, the plan was to rebuild Iraq’s entire political system so it would never threaten its neighbors or Israel again. These specific goals dictated almost every move the Coalition Provisional Authority made while they ran the country during the occupation.

Order Number 1 dissolved the Baath Party and stripped its members of their government jobs. Order Number 2 disbanded the Iraqi Army and the country’s intelligence services. These moves tore down the institutional framework that held the Iraqi state together for decades. De-Baathification purged tens of thousands of civil servants and military officers. Many of these people joined the insurgency that started boiling over in 2003 and 2004. The disbanded army left a massive group of trained men without work, but they still had their weapons and military expertise. These choices showed the Clean Break preference for a total rupture with the past.

Restructuring went deep. The Coalition Provisional Authority set up a federal system centered on ethnic and sectarian lines. Kurdish autonomy in the north gained constitutional protection. The Kurdistan Regional Government became a semi-autonomous power with its own military, the Peshmerga. Shi’a parties took over the central government in Baghdad. Sunni Arabs felt pushed to the edges even though they ran the show under Saddam Hussein. This fragmentation fit the Clean Break vision of a weakened Iraq. A divided nation cannot mount a unified military threat against Israel or its neighbors.

Paul Bremer ran the Coalition Provisional Authority. His words were law during the occupation. Bremer took his cues from Pentagon leaders like Donald Rumsfeld and Paul Wolfowitz. The orders to dissolve the army and purge the Baathists came from Washington. Field commanders who actually understood the ground in Iraq were largely ignored. General Jay Garner originally led the reconstruction but opposed these harsh measures. Bremer replaced him just weeks after the invasion. This swap made it clear that political goals mattered more than actual stability.

The humanitarian fallout was staggering. Estimates show over one million Iraqis died from the direct and indirect effects of the war. Three million people fled their homes. Infrastructure that survived years of sanctions and the 1991 Gulf War finally gave out under looting and neglect. Electricity and water systems failed to hit pre-war levels for years. The insurgency hit coalition troops and civilians alike. This sparked sectarian violence that spiraled into a full-scale civil war by 2006.

The authors of Clean Break ignored these risks in their 1996 memo. That document focused on strategic goals and skipped the messy details of implementation. It is a common hole in neoconservative strategy. They treated regime change like a single event. They assumed removing a dictator would naturally lead to a stable democracy. That was wrong. The resulting power vacuum invited extremist groups like Al-Qaeda in Iraq. This group eventually became ISIS. By 2014, ISIS controlled huge parts of Iraq and Syria. It was a bigger threat than the regime they replaced.

The Kurdish situation shows another blind spot. Clean Break wanted to empower Kurds to weaken the central Iraqi state. This created a pro-Western pocket in the north, but it also poisoned the relationship with Turkey. Turkish leaders see Kurdish autonomy as a threat to their own borders. They worry a Kurdish state in Iraq will fuel separatism at home. This tension defined much of the occupation and still messes with American-Turkish relations today. The Clean Break authors fixed one problem but created several new ones.

### **Syria’s Turn in the Crosshairs**

The Clean Break document essentially framed Syria as a wall that needed to be knocked down. The people behind the report looked at Damascus and Tehran and saw a bridge forming between the Persian Gulf and the Mediterranean. In their eyes, Israel could only be safe if the Assad regime was pushed into a corner or dismantled entirely. While the United States skipped a full-scale invasion like the one in Iraq, the mindset from that 1996 report stuck around. It colored how Washington handled Damascus for decades. By the time the Syrian Civil War kicked off in 2011, “regime change” had gone from a policy paper theory to a loud, constant demand.

Things shifted fast after Iraq fell. With Saddam Hussein gone, the old plan of keeping both Iraq and Iran in a box fell apart. Syria was suddenly the last secular Arab state standing with Iran against American and Israeli goals. The Bush administration started leaning on them hard. They funded opposition groups and slapped on sanctions to choke the economy. It was a clear attempt to make the ground shake under the government. These moves look a lot like the “maximum pressure” tactics we see used against Iran today.

When the civil war actually hit, Syria turned into a playground for proxy fights. Iran and Russia jumped in to keep Assad in his seat. On the other side, a messy mix of countries like Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Qatar poured resources into different rebel groups, often with conflicting goals. The Clean Break authors were worried about conflict spreading, and they were right about that part, even if it didn’t go the way they hoped. The war blew up in everyone’s face. It created a massive humanitarian disaster with six million people fleeing the country and millions more losing their homes inside the borders.

Instead of breaking the bond between Tehran and Damascus, the pressure actually fused them together. Since Iraq was no longer a threat to Syria’s east, Assad could focus on holding his ground and tightening his ties with Iran. Syria became the main highway for Iranian influence to reach the Mediterranean. Hezbollah and other militias backed by Iran started using Syrian soil to post up right on Israel’s northern border. The whole idea of weakening the regime to bring “democracy” backfired. It ended up building a much tougher, more dangerous alliance than what existed before the Iraq war started.

By 2019, the Trump administration pulled American troops out of northern Syria. That left a hole that Turkey immediately filled with its own military. This move fit the Clean Break logic of using local players to destabilize a target rather than keeping American boots on the ground. The Kurdish-led Syrian Democratic Forces, who had been the main partners in fighting ISIS, were basically left to deal with the Turkish onslaught alone. It was a cold calculation to keep the pressure on Assad’s borders without a long-term US commitment.

The pressure reached a peak with the Caesar Syria Civilian Protection Act passed by Congress in 2019. These sanctions were designed to be a total blockade, punishing anyone who dared to trade with the Syrian government. On paper, it was supposed to force Assad to the negotiating table. In reality, the people at the top stayed in power thanks to Russian and Iranian help. The folks actually feeling the pain were the civilians. Food, medicine, and building materials for homes became incredibly hard to find. The policy did what it was supposed to do by keeping the pressure high, but the political change it promised never actually happened.

### **Lebanon and the Hezbollah Challenge**

The 2006 Second Lebanon War wasn’t just another border skirmish. It was the moment Israel finally walked away from decades of trying to simply “contain” Hezbollah. Everything changed when Hezbollah fighters crossed the blue line, ambushed a patrol, and took two Israeli soldiers captive. Israel didn’t just push back. They launched a massive campaign of air strikes and naval shelling before sending ground troops deep into southern Lebanese territory. It was a clear message. The goal was to tear down Hezbollah’s military setup and hit their leaders hard. This war set a much more violent precedent for how Israel would handle threats on that front moving forward.

The logic behind this mirrored the ideas in the “Clean Break” paper. That document argued that groups backed by Iran were all part of the same dangerous web stretching from Beirut to Damascus and Tehran. It suggested Israel take the lead by directly hitting Hezbollah and Syria. When the 2006 war broke out, Israel put those theories into practice. Instead of sticking to the quiet, shadow-war tactics the memo originally floated, they used full-scale military power. The Israeli Air Force flew thousands of missions, pounding targets from the southern hills all the way to the neighborhoods of Beirut.

The war wrapped up without actually hitting its main targets. Hezbollah kept its weapons and its seat at the political table in Lebanon. Hassan Nasrallah even claimed a win, though Lebanese infrastructure took a massive hit. The numbers tell a grim story. Over a thousand Lebanese civilians died in those thirty-four days. On the other side, the conflict killed one hundred sixty Israeli soldiers and forty-three civilians. It forced a million people in Lebanon out of their homes. Entire neighborhoods, power plants, roads, and bridges turned to rubble.

Post-war life just doubled down on the hardline tactics used during the fighting. Israel and the U.S. stayed focused on boxing in Iranian influence. They did this by backing specific Lebanese politicians, keeping a constant eye on Hezbollah’s growing arsenal, and slapping sanctions on anyone tied to the group. It is a strategy ripped straight from the Clean Break document. This approach basically treats constant tension as the baseline. It skips over diplomacy in favor of a permanent standoff.

Hezbollah actually got much better at gathering intelligence after the IDF left South Lebanon in 2000. It is a never-ending cycle. One side moves, the other tries to outsmart them. The fact that Hezbollah is still a powerhouse proves that you can’t just bully your way out of a deep security mess. Even recent wins, like the U.S.-backed maritime deal that set a formal border between Israel and Lebanon, don’t change the big picture. Hezbollah still has a massive stockpile of rockets. Lebanon shows exactly how stubborn an enemy can be. It proves that military force has a hard time delivering real, lasting peace.

### **Iran and the Maximum Pressure Campaign**

The way American leaders handle Iran today is basically a live demonstration of those Clean Break ideas from decades ago. That 1996 paper called Iran the biggest long-term danger to Israel and the rest of the region. The people who wrote it thought the old plan of just chatting or trying to keep them quiet was a total waste of time. They wanted to get aggressive. Their idea was to use local partners or other groups to jump in and start rolling back Tehran’s influence. All that tough talk from years ago gave future presidents a blueprint for dealing with Iran’s nukes and its reach across the borders.

Clinton went with a plan called dual containment. He wanted to keep both Iran and Iraq stuck in a corner to keep the region quiet. But the Clean Break group thought that was a mistake. They felt containment just let bad regimes hang around for too long. Everything changed after the 2003 invasion of Iraq. With Iraq out of the way, the US focused almost entirely on Iran. They started blaming Iran for most of the trouble in the Persian Gulf. This was a jump from sitting on the sidelines to looking for a fight. The urge to break an enemy outweighed the old habit of watching them. This became the standard way of doing business after the Iraq War ended.

Trump’s maximum pressure campaign was the most extreme version of this mindset. He decided to crush the entire Iranian economy. The goal was to force them to rewrite the nuclear deal and change how they acted. This involved putting massive sanctions back on the table. They went after oil sales, the banks, shipping routes, and even the Revolutionary Guard. It was a total economic war. The whole point was to cause enough pain that the government in Tehran would finally feel forced to make a deal.

The U.S. slapped sanctions on 970 different Iranian entities during that maximum pressure run. Officials rolled out 26 separate rounds of these penalties. They hit basically every corner of the Iranian economy. It worked if you only look at the oil numbers. Exports plummeted from about 2.5 million barrels every day down to less than 500,000 when the squeeze was tightest. The local currency took a total nose-dive against the dollar. Inflation spiked to levels people hadn’t dealt with since the 1980s. Regular Iranians felt the brunt of this. Even though they needed medicine and medical gear, banking blocks muddied the waters for international deals. Essential goods became hard to find.

But the whole plan missed its mark. Iran kept their nuclear program running. Tensions across the region actually spiked. Groups backed by Iran stayed busy all over the Middle East. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps even grew their footprint in places like Syria. They stayed active in Iraq and Yemen while keeping a strong presence in Lebanon. They did all this while the sanctions were in full swing. Iran pushed their nuclear enrichment closer to weapons-grade levels than before the pressure started. This approach backfired. It gave more power to the hardliners in Tehran. Those leaders argued that trying to play nice with the West only leads to more pain regardless of what Iran actually does.

Biden’s team tried to get back into the nuclear deal from the Obama years. Those talks hit a wall. Iran wanted promises that sanctions relief would actually stick this time. The White House had its own political headaches at home to navigate. The fallout from the maximum pressure era made any new diplomacy feel impossible. Trust was gone. Iran saw the American word as worthless after the Trump administration walked away from the original pact. At the same time, negotiators in D.C. faced a lot of heat from people who wanted a deal to cover more than just nukes.

### **Libya as Parallel Case Study**

Look at the 2011 NATO push into Libya. It perfectly mirrors that Clean Break strategy of swapping out regimes, even if there isn’t a direct paper trail. Western leaders saw Muammar Qaddafi as a total wrecking ball for the region. He was a tyrant. Revolutionaries and their foreign allies wanted him gone. So, NATO launched a campaign backed by UN Resolution 1973. They used airstrikes. They backed the rebels. They claimed they were just protecting people. The actual result was the fast collapse and death of the Qaddafi government.

This move captures the core of the Clean Break philosophy. You take out an enemy leader and assume a new political system will just appear. The people in charge had total faith that military power could force a specific political result. But they ignored the danger of skipping the planning stage for what comes after. When the government crumbled, everything else did too. A massive power vacuum opened up. Factions started tearing the country apart. It became a long war between different militias.

Libya highlights the gap between breaking a system and actually running a country. Erasing a government creates a mess. The intervention triggered a total state collapse and a humanitarian nightmare. It remains a disaster today. The country turned into a jumping-off point for migrants heading to Europe. And Qaddafi’s weapons leaked out into the Sahel. Those guns fueled fighting in places like Mali. Terrorist cells set up shop because no one was there to keep the peace.

The original Clean Break authors didn’t mention Libya in their 1996 report. Still, the thinking is the same as their plans for Iraq. Foreign powers pick a target. They use force to remove it. They hope things get better once the old guard is gone. Libya proved that idea is a failure. It reveals a deep ignorance about how a functioning society actually works.

### **The Sykes-Picot Parallel and Historical Continuity**

The Clean Break doctrine isn’t some isolated modern theory. It actually echoes old historical patterns of outsiders messing with the Middle East, specifically the 1916 Sykes-Picot Agreement. If you look closely, there is a clear line connecting these two frameworks even though eighty years sit between them. Sykes-Picot grew out of imperial competition. Clean Break sprang from a specific ideological movement after the Cold War ended. Yet, both show powerful countries trying to redraw maps from the top down based on their own strategic math. They both ignore local sovereignty. They treat existing borders as suggestions and prioritize what is convenient for their own national interests.

Britain and France signed Sykes-Picot in secret. They split up Ottoman Arab lands into spheres of influence like they were dividing a pie. That single moment saw foreign diplomats decide the fate of millions, carving up a continent and planting the seeds for a century of identity crises and border wars. Clean Break pushes for a similar radical overhaul. It wants external players, mainly Israel and the U.S., to restructure the region to fit their goals. The authors didn’t literally draw lines on a physical map the way Sykes and Picot did. Instead, they proposed a new geopolitical architecture that relies on dismantling existing states and alliances.

Both plans lean heavily toward breaking states apart and weakening sovereign power. Sykes-Picot sliced Ottoman territory into British and French zones, creating puppet states that never had real independence. Those lines were artificial. They ignored ethnic, religious, and tribal realities. This created a volatile environment that has been burning ever since. Clean Break tries to reach those same ends using different tools. It aims to fracture states by feeding internal divisions rather than using colonial decrees. For example, the vision for Iraq after an invasion involved empowering sectarian groups to build a weak, divided federation. The goal was simple. Ensure Iraq could never function as a single, powerful military force again.

The way the Islamic State talked about the end of Sykes-Picot provides a weirdly helpful mirror here. ISIS framed its violence as a war against the colonial borders imposed in 1916. They wanted a transnational caliphate to erase those old lines. They were essentially fighting to bring back the world as it looked before the Europeans arrived. Clean Break isn’t trying to burn every border or go back to medieval times. It just wants a total departure from the political realities of the post-1916 state system. It aims to wipe away the strategic alignments and sovereign rights that have stood for a hundred years. The idea is to replace them with a new, fluid map that is easier to control. Sykes-Picot was about carving up physical land. Clean Break is about carving up political power.

### **The Palestinian Policy Shift**

The Clean Break paper hit Israeli policy like a sledgehammer. When Benjamin Netanyahu took over as Likud leader right before his 1996 win, this report gave him the intellectual ammo he needed to walk away from the Oslo Accords. It basically laid the tracks for the “BiBi Doctrine”. That strategy swaps out old-school diplomacy for raw military power, more settlements, and a total refusal to budge unless security is absolute. The authors didn’t mince words. They claimed the whole land-for-peace idea was a bust that only rewarded violence and invited more attacks.

The timing here was everything. The report landed right in the middle of a campaign, acting as a blueprint for a new kind of government. These ideas fit the Likud platform perfectly because the party never really trusted the Oslo process to begin with. The paper attacked the fundamental logic of those agreements. It argued that the Accords hadn’t brought peace but had instead given a leg up to the Palestinian Authority and various militant groups. By describing the situation as a permanent struggle against a forever-hostile neighbor, the report gave Netanyahu a solid excuse to stop making concessions.

Once in power, the new administration moved fast to ramp up building projects in the West Bank and East Jerusalem. This flew right in the face of the Oslo agreements, which were supposed to freeze that kind of activity. Suddenly, building settlements became a central pillar of Israel’s long-term security plan. This shift made giving up land in the future almost impossible from a political standpoint. The numbers tell the story clearly. The settler population in these areas jumped from about 150,000 in 1996 to over 500,000 by 2020. This massive demographic shift makes the idea of a two-state solution look more like a logistical nightmare than a reality.

The Clean Break paper shifted how Israel handled security and its military moves against Palestinian targets. It wasn’t a minor change. By choosing raw deterrence over the old habit of negotiating, the government moved toward a policy of applying maximum pressure. This shift hit hard during the Second Intifada. When that conflict broke out right after Netanyahu took office, the military response turned much more aggressive. It was punitive. The army started moving with more lethality, and the hit lists for Hamas and Fatah leaders grew longer. You could see the departure from the more restrained years that came before. The new mindset favored using overwhelming force to break resistance instead of talking through the underlying grievances.

Essentially, the report gave the green light to a strategy of managing the conflict through pure strength. It leaned into the idea that a permanent state of tension might actually be the only realistic outcome for the long haul. People started calling this the Netanyahu Doctrine. It prioritized taking one-sided steps to beef up security. They built the separation barrier. They sent troops deep into Palestinian cities for massive operations. None of this relied on bilateral agreements or the typical back-and-forth of concessions. The document gave a whole generation of leaders the words and the logic they needed to walk away from a negotiated two-state solution. They traded that old dream for a strategy built on control, scaring off opponents, and tightening their demographic grip.

### **The Human Cost Buried in Strategic Language**

The Clean Break document relies on sterile language that effectively masks the human suffering its strategies cause. It frames regime change as a cold strategic goal. It completely ignores the civilian body counts that inevitably follow military invasions. Breaking up states is sold as a way to improve security. But this ignores the brutal ethnic and sectarian slaughter that actually happens when political systems fall apart. Then you have maximum pressure campaigns. These are labeled as economic tools. In reality, they mean ordinary people can’t find medicine or food.

The numbers from the Iraq invasion are staggering. Research from Johns Hopkins and other groups shows that over a million people died. Three million Iraqis lost their homes. Critical systems for power, clean water, and sewage just collapsed. This left millions in the dark. Everyday life became a nightmare of kidnappings, bombings, and militia hits. You won’t find a single mention of these calculations in the Clean Break text.

Syria tells a similar, horrific story. Over 500,000 people were killed. More than half the population had to flee. Six million people ended up as refugees in other countries, while millions more wandered their own borders internally. Syria turned into a playground for international powers. They fought their battles through local proxies. We all remember the photos of drowned children on beaches that briefly woke up the world’s conscience. That is what happens when you try to topple a regime like Assad’s without any real thought for the chaos that follows.

The 2006 war left Lebanon in ruins. Thirty-four days of fighting killed over a thousand people and forced a million others to flee their homes. Bombs hit everything. Bridges, roads, power plants, and entire neighborhoods crumbled. Fixing it all cost billions. Even after the smoke cleared, the original security problems stuck around because Hezbollah kept its military strength. The threat that started the war never really went away.

In Iran, ordinary people feel the weight of “maximum pressure” sanctions every day. It is getting harder to find basic medicine and hospital equipment. Banking rules make it nearly impossible to pay for humanitarian imports from other countries. Because the currency lost so much value, people can’t afford what they used to. Inflation is the worst it has been since the eighties. These sanctions were supposed to squeeze the leadership, but the government just uses oil smuggling to stay in power while the public suffers the fallout.

Palestinians have watched their chances for a state slip away as settlement construction and military raids ramped up. This shift followed the logic of Clean Break principles in Israeli policy. Constant building in the West Bank breaks up the land, making a continuous Palestinian territory look unlikely. On top of that, major military operations in Gaza, spanning from 2008 all the way to 2026, left thousands dead and leveled the infrastructure. Between the new buildings and the political gridlock, the idea of a two-state solution feels further off than ever.

### **The Accountability Gap**

The Clean Break authors never acknowledged responsibility for the consequences that followed implementation of their recommendations. Richard Perle resigned from the Defense Policy Board in 2003 amid controversy over his business connections and role in advocating for the Iraq War. He did not express regret for the policy itself. Douglas Feith left the Defense Department in 2005 and wrote a memoir defending his role in Iraq War planning. He maintained that the intelligence supporting the war was sound despite subsequent findings to the contrary. David Wurmser continued working in conservative policy circles without addressing the FBI counterintelligence investigation into his handling of classified information.

The lack of accountability reflects broader patterns in foreign policy decision-making. Officials who advocate for wars face few personal consequences when those wars produce disastrous outcomes. They retain positions in think tanks, write books, give lectures, and advise subsequent administrations. The human costs of their policy recommendations are borne by others. Soldiers who fought in Iraq and Afghanistan returned with physical and psychological injuries. Civilians in Iraq, Syria, Libya, and elsewhere died or lost their homes. Regional instability created conditions for extremist groups to flourish.

Congressional investigations produced reports documenting intelligence failures and policy mistakes, but no officials faced criminal charges for misleading the public about Iraq’s weapons of mass destruction. The Pentagon Inspector General’s investigation into the Office of Special Plans documented problematic practices without recommending prosecutions. The 9/11 Commission found no credible evidence of Iraq-Al Qaeda operational cooperation, contradicting administration claims used to justify the war. These findings did not produce accountability for officials who made those claims.

The think tank ecosystem that produced Clean Break continues to generate policy recommendations without mechanisms for evaluating past predictions. The Institute for Advanced Strategic and Political Studies remains active. The Project for the New American Century dissolved in 2006 after achieving many of its objectives, but its personnel dispersed to other organizations where they continue influencing policy. The American Enterprise Institute, Heritage Foundation, and other “conservative” think tanks maintain staff who advocated for the Iraq War and continue promoting interventionist policies.

The Network That Sustained Influence
The Clean Break crew stayed relevant through several administrations by grabbing seats in think tanks and media outlets. William Kristol is the perfect example. He ran The Weekly Standard and turned it into a primary hub for neoconservative thought during the 1990s and 2000s. The paper beat the drum for the Iraq War. It stayed vocal about intervention long after the initial invasion. Kristol was everywhere on TV. He used those news spots to frame the national conversation on foreign policy.

Then you have Richard Perle. He sat on the Defense Policy Board. He skipped the traditional cabinet route. Still, he walked the halls of the Pentagon with ease. This board gives the Secretary of Defense a second opinion. It offers a shortcut to the highest levels of the defense world. Perle used this access to pitch the hardline strategies found in the Clean Break document. He made these aggressive ideas sound urgent to military leaders. His name stood for a specific kind of hawkishness. That made his arguments for preemptive strikes and regime change feel heavy and serious.

Douglas Feith worked from the inside as the Under Secretary of Defense for Policy. He occupied the center of the planning for the Iraq War. His office dictated the strategic posture of the whole department. He was also smart about human resources. Feith placed his ideological allies in spots where they could do the most good for his cause. This built a self-sustaining circle of influence. And he picked Stephen Cambone to be his deputy. It was a deliberate play to stock the Pentagon with people who would carry out his specific vision.

David Wurmser landed a spot as an advisor to Dick Cheney. This move put him right in the middle of the White House national security team. He had the Vice President’s ear. This access allowed him to steer how the administration viewed Iraq and the Middle East. Wurmser also took his message to the public. He talked about regime change in very blunt terms. These appearances made the idea of a massive political overhaul sound normal. He stayed ahead of the critics who worried about messy consequences.

Paul Wolfowitz was the number two guy at the Pentagon. Since he was tight with Donald Rumsfeld and President Bush, he had massive pull regarding military strategy. Everyone knew he wanted to use the military to flip the Middle East on its head. He was the main guy pushing for the Iraq invasion. Wolfowitz and Douglas Feith represented a new wave of thinkers. They started on the fringes. They eventually ended up running the show.

All this concentrated power ensured the Clean Break strategy stayed relevant. It functioned as a living guide for actual decisions. This group made the radical ideas from 1996 look like realistic options. They went after their goals with total focus. The network illustrated the impact of a tight-knit group of believers when they get their hands on the steering wheel of government power.

### **The Ideological Synthesis**

Clean Break was essentially a mashup of neoconservatism and a very particular brand of Zionism. The paper calls for a complete overhaul of the Middle East map to tip the scales for Israel. This mix created a mindset that surpassed the reach of either group on its own. It turned aggressive intervention into a dual-purpose goal: a global fight for democracy and a specific Jewish national requirement.

Neoconservatism provided the fuel and the vocabulary for the whole plan. The people behind it were mostly liberal intellectuals who got fed up with the soft diplomacy and the 1960s counterculture. They built a worldview based on a hatred for communism. They viewed diplomatic compromises as a trap. They also believed the U.S. military could solve political problems and spread democracy across the globe. Because of this, the demand for regime change in Clean Break is pure neoconservatism. It is a total departure from the cautious, step-by-step foreign policy of the mid-20th century.

The “Clean Break” document gave Zionism a specific set of maps and political targets. The ultimate goal was a Jewish state that felt truly secure. They wanted maximalist borders. And the authors argued that survival required aggressive regional action. Instead, they pushed for a total overhaul of the surrounding area to ensure Israel’s survival for the long haul. This fits the “Greater Israel” ideology perfectly. These thinkers value security and land over the trade-offs found in the Oslo Accords or Labor Zionism.

The real innovation of “Clean Break” was how it fused two different ideologies into one strategy. It took the neoconservative drive to intervene in foreign affairs and aimed it at Israel’s specific security goals. The writers framed the fight against hostile Arab regimes as an absolute necessity for Israel’s survival. This logic made the transformation of the Middle East seem like a core American interest. This overlap of interests is a major theme in US foreign policy studies.

Critics like John Mearsheimer and Stephen Walt analyze this shift. They argue this combination created a powerful cycle where the Israel lobby and neoconservative groups reinforced each other’s plans. They believe this steered American policy in a pro-Israel direction that damaged US interests. From this view, the “Clean Break” strategy shows how specific political goals get wrapped in language about spreading democracy to gain support. The strategy argued that helping Israel achieve a clean break from its enemies would advance American strategic interests and create a stable, democratic region.

### **The Pattern Across Administrations**

The Clean Break influence reached well past the Bush years and shaped how the next presidents handled the world. Take the Obama administration. They shifted toward diplomacy by hammering out the Iran nuclear deal and pulling troops from Iraq. Yet, those same leaders leaned hard on drone strikes and special operations. These moves followed the old playbook of preemptive action. In Syria, they backed opposition groups to keep the pressure on the Assad regime. They avoided a full military invasion but kept the heat on through these proxy fights. These patterns of intervention proved much harder to break than the campaign speeches suggested.

The Trump years saw a hard pivot back to “maximum pressure” on Iran. By ditching the nuclear deal and slapping sanctions back on, the administration signaled they were done with waiting. It wasn’t just economic, either. They launched military strikes against Iranian targets and militias across Iraq and Syria. Then came the 2020 killing of General Qasem Soleimani. That move was a massive escalation. It showed a clear preference for the Clean Break philosophy, choosing direct confrontation over the old goal of containment.

Biden tried to shift gears toward diplomacy, but those efforts mostly hit a wall. Iranian demands and messy domestic politics in the U.S. kept things stalled. So, the administration kept the sanctions in place while they talked. Over in Syria, American troops stayed put. This happened despite all those campaign promises to wrap up “forever wars.” They kept backing Kurdish forces to fight what’s left of ISIS, all while trying to balance the needs of Turkey and other local players.

This overlap between two very different presidents suggests something deeper. It looks like Clean Break principles actually got baked into the D.C. bureaucracy. The people who stick around through every election ended up carrying out a strategy born in the Bush era. Think tanks kept churning out papers that favored stepping in, and the media stayed locked on military threats. Diplomacy just didn’t get the same spotlight.

### **The Strategic Miscalculation**

Clean Break was built on a series of guesses about political change that simply didn’t hold up. The authors figured that if you yanked a hostile leader out of power, a better government would just naturally slide into place. But they looked at these regimes like they were just one-man shows. They weren’t. These governments were rooted in deep social and economic realities that didn’t vanish when the dictator did. Taking out Saddam Hussein didn’t hand the world a democratic Iraq that loved American interests. Instead, it cracked open a door for sectarian civil war and gave Iran a massive amount of room to run. It eventually paved the way for ISIS to rise.

There was also this strange confidence that outsiders could engineer a whole new political landscape without everything blowing up. It was a massive underestimation of how tough local social structures really are. When those regimes collapsed, the resulting power vacuums didn’t stay empty for long. Actors far more dangerous than the original targets moved in. Extremist groups grabbed land and cash while regional rivals got a lot more influential. In the middle of it all, regular people paid the price through constant violence and losing their homes.

The document also banked on the idea that American and Israeli interests were a perfect match. It assumed that chasing Israel’s security goals would automatically help the U.S. national interest too. This line of thinking ignored the very real friction between the strategic priorities of the two countries. Getting tangled in Middle East wars cost the United States an incredible amount of blood and money, which eventually sparked a huge political backlash at home. These conflicts hurt American credibility on the world stage. They also sucked away resources that could have gone toward other big issues, like the growing competition with China.

Finally, there was a belief that guns and bombs could finish the job where diplomacy had stalled. It treated military force as a superior shortcut for political transformation. But the actual results showed that military power has some pretty hard limits when it comes to building something lasting. An occupation force can topple a building or an army, sure. But it can’t force a stable government to grow out of the rubble. Having the best military in the world might win the battle, but it doesn’t solve the underlying political fights that started the war in the first place.

### **Conclusion**

The Clean Break document transformed from six-page policy memo into operational blueprint for two decades of Middle East intervention. Its authors occupied positions that allowed direct implementation of their recommendations. The Iraq invasion, Syrian pressure campaign, Lebanon War, and Iran sanctions all carry fingerprints traceable to that 1996 text. The human costs accumulated through civilian deaths, displacement, and regional instability. The strategic assumptions underlying the document proved incorrect. Regime change produced chaos rather than stability. Military force achieved tactical objectives but failed strategically. The accountability gap remains unaddressed. Officials who advocated for these policies face no personal consequences while others bear the costs. The legacy continues shaping contemporary foreign policy despite demonstrated failures. Future decision-makers should examine this documentary record before embracing similar frameworks that promise transformation through force while obscuring the human consequences buried in strategic language.
