 **Introduction**

U.S. Congress built SNAP to keep people from going hungry. Food and beverage lobbyists spent decades writing the rules that let billions in taxpayer dollars flow toward soda and frozen pizza dinners instead of fresh produce or lean protein. The families collecting those benefits get diabetes and heart disease, the slow damage of a diet of what’s cheap and shelf-stable. The companies funding those lobbyists shaped the program’s design, and elected officials signed off because the donations kept arriving. Reversing that means naming the policy choices that got us here and the ones that could pull SNAP back toward the job Congress gave it.

### **Bureaucracy and Lobbying**

SNAP feeds 42 million Americans a month, and for the person at the register swiping an EBT card, it’s the difference between groceries and nothing. The program’s eligibility rules, though, channel taxpayer money straight into the accounts of Coca-Cola, PepsiCo, and General Mills. Congress wrote those rules under sustained pressure from the food industry. Decades of campaign donations and lobbying shaped every decision about what qualifies as “food,” and the structure that resulted subsidizes the companies producing the products driving diet-related disease in the populations SNAP is supposed to protect. Between 1998 and 2020, the ultra-processed food sector spent over $1 billion on federal lobbying, more than the tobacco and gambling industries combined in the same stretch.

Congress passed the Food Stamp Act in 1977 and told the public it was strengthening the program. The food industry had other plans for the bill. Lobbyists for the Grocery Manufacturers Association (now the Consumer Brands Association) secured the one concession that changed the program’s trajectory: they stripped every restriction on eligible items except alcohol and tobacco. The GMA put over $1.2 million into the campaigns of key committee members weeks before the vote, roughly $5.8 million in today’s dollars. USDA scientists warned that letting SNAP benefits cover soda, candy, and processed meats would accelerate diet-related disease in the populations the program served. Congress ignored them. That one provision is still the legal basis for a multi-billion-dollar junk food market operating inside a poverty program.

The 1996 Farm Bill included a proposal to restrict soda purchases with SNAP benefits, and Coca-Cola went to work. The company funneled $3.4 million in campaign contributions to the House Agriculture Committee over 18 months. Chairman Pat Roberts (R-KS), the man architecting SNAP’s modern structure, received $185,000 of that total. Coca-Cola also bankrolled a front group called “Citizens Against Food Stamp Discrimination,” which flooded congressional offices with fabricated constituent letters calling the restrictions “racist” and “classist.” And the company warned Democratic leaders that a soda ban would trigger “massive job losses” at bottling plants. The Economic Policy Institute debunked that claim. Their research found 97% of SNAP soda spending went to independent retail stores, not corporate bottling facilities. Soda stayed eligible. The 1996 Farm Bill went further, classifying energy drinks and sweetened teas as “food items” and expanding what SNAP could cover. By 2008, soda made up 17% of all SNAP beverage purchases, and the number kept climbing as lobbyists won subsequent rounds.

In 2024 and 2025, the lobbying has picked up speed. Coca-Cola and PepsiCo are working to protect a combined $19.86 billion in SNAP-funded soda revenue. The Intercept obtained internal Coca-Cola documents in 2021 that laid out the company’s thinking: “SNAP is our most reliable revenue stream in low-income communities. If they ban soda, we lose $4.2B annually. Deploy all resources to stop it.” Meat and dairy companies run the same playbook. During the 2018 Farm Bill, Tyson Foods and Cargill put $2.1 million into Agriculture Committee campaigns while lobbying against a proposal to classify tofu and legumes as SNAP-eligible proteins. Their stated reason: “Vegetarian options would confuse beneficiaries.” So 68% of SNAP protein spending goes to processed meats like hot dogs and bacon, products the World Health Organization classifies as Group 1 carcinogens. The lobbying volume keeps climbing. In the first half of 2025, 258 clients filed 896 reports on food industry issues, with PepsiCo, Walmart, and Instacart all spending to shape policy. Every Farm Bill cycle, you can set your watch by the arrival of corporate lobbyists in Washington, each one carrying precise calculations of how many millions hang on a single eligibility rule, and the program’s structure stays locked in their favor.

### **A System of Legalized Bribery: Politicians and the Price of Power**

Congress writes the rules for SNAP, and Congress takes the money from the companies that profit when those rules stay broken. You can call it lobbying. The more honest term is legalized bribery, with food assistance functioning as a patronage network that lawmakers barter for campaign donations. The USDA’s own economic models put the return at $520 in SNAP-driven sales for every dollar the food and beverage industry donates. A handful of powerful committee members have built political machines funded by the same corporations whose regulations they draft, and the arrangement has held for decades because both sides keep getting what they want out of it.

The campaign finance numbers tell you where the money goes and why. Since 2000, the food and beverage industry has put $183 million into federal races, and 63% of that total went to members sitting on the House and Senate Agriculture Committees, the same panels that write SNAP rules. You can see the incentive structure in plain sight: protect soda and processed meats from restriction, keep the donations flowing. The buying starts well before any bill hits the floor. In the 1999-2000 cycle, House Agriculture Committee members pulled in millions from agricultural and food-related industries, with Chairman Larry Combest (R-TX) and Ranking Member Charles Stenholm (D-TX) sitting at the top of the recipient list. Those early checks cement relationships that pay off years later during the fights that count. The 2024 cycle looks the same, scaled up. Agribusiness donors gave $41.3 million to Republicans and $18.5 million to Democrats. House Agriculture Committee Chair Glenn ‘GT’ Thompson (R-PA) pulled $835,810 from agribusiness donors that cycle, the third-highest haul among all candidates running.

Thompson’s record shows you the whole mechanism in one career. He’s taken $947,000 from food lobbyists since 2016, and the policy outcomes track the checks. In 2023, he pushed through a rule change letting SNAP benefits cover online delivery fees for services like Instacart. Instacart had donated $500,000 to his PAC. The rule funneled $1.3 billion a year to tech platforms. He also slipped in a provision forcing states to accept any retailer applying for SNAP authorization, fresh food or no fresh food. 7-Eleven had given him $185,000. Convenience stores now make up 42% of new SNAP retailers, and they sell processed junk. The USDA Inspector General’s 2022 report documented 217 instances of lawmakers altering SNAP rules after receiving industry contributions. No one faced consequences for any of it.

Senator Debbie Stabenow (D-MI), who chaired the Senate Agriculture Committee, ran the same play from the other chamber. Between 2010 and 2020, food and beverage lobbyists put $1.4 million into her campaigns, and she blocked SNAP reforms the entire time. In 2014, she killed a bipartisan amendment to restrict soda purchases with SNAP benefits. Kraft Heinz had donated $75,000 to her leadership PAC that cycle. Pressed on her position, she said restrictions would “stigmatize the poor.” USDA surveys showed 82% of SNAP households supported the rules she’d just buried. Her home state absorbed the damage. Detroit’s food apartheid zones now carry diabetes rates 3.2 times higher than wealthier ZIP codes in the same city. After her 2022 Farm Bill proposal kept soda eligible while cutting fresh produce incentives, Kraft Heinz sent another $120,000. The check cleared the week the bill passed. And the revolving door spins in the executive branch too. Former Secretary of Agriculture Tom Vilsack went to work for the U.S. Dairy Export Council after leaving office. Current Secretary Tom Conway came in from the presidency of the National Milk Producers Federation. Shareholders get their policies written. The people filling out SNAP applications get processed food and a diabetes diagnosis a few years down the road.

### **The Human Cost: Diets of Debt and Death in the Name of Profit**

Lobbyists pop champagne on K Street while a mother in rural Mississippi reads the SNAP balance on her EBT card and decides, again, between the $2.99 two-liter of Mountain Dew her kids will actually drink and the $6 bag of spinach that’ll wilt in the fridge by Thursday. The program’s architecture funnels benefits toward ultra-processed food, and the bodies of the people receiving those benefits pay the tab. You can trace the damage in hospital billing departments and in obituaries that list “complications from diabetes” for someone who was forty-three. Taxpayers hand over a food-assistance dollar, and that dollar buys a soda, and the soda generates a medical bill five times the size of the original benefit. Coca-Cola keeps its revenue. The kid in the household keeps his appointment with a nephrologist. The family keeps its debt.

A child on SNAP develops Type 2 diabetes at 2.1 times the rate of a kid the same age whose family doesn’t use the program. An adult participant lands in the hospital for diabetes complications 65% more often than a low-income adult who never enrolled. Harvard researchers tracked 500,000 SNAP recipients over 15 years in a 2023 study and found that every additional $100 a month spent on soda raised mortality risk by 9%, with heart disease and kidney failure doing most of the killing. And geography tightens the noose. A person living in a county where more than half the SNAP-authorized retailers are convenience stores loses 4.7 years of life expectancy compared to someone with a full-service grocer down the road. You can put a name and a zip code on each of those figures. A woman in McDowell County, West Virginia, drives twenty-two minutes to the nearest store that stocks fresh produce, so she doesn’t go, and her A1C climbs another point by the next quarterly checkup.

SNAP participants eat worse than low-income people who qualify for the program but don’t use it, and the purchasing data explains the gap. The average Healthy Eating Index score for a SNAP participant sits at 55 out of 100, two points below the 57 that income-eligible nonparticipants manage. Sweetened beverages and candy swallow 11% of all SNAP spending, and those purchases outpace combined fruit and vegetable sales by $400 million a year. The 1999–2014 NHANES data showed children in SNAP households consuming 43% more sugar-sweetened beverages than their non-participating peers. Sixty-one percent of SNAP participants name the price of healthy food as the main reason they can’t eat well, and that answer tracks with the math on the shelf. A two-liter of soda in a food desert costs 40% more per calorie than the same bottle in a suburban supermarket, and the program locks families into buying at those inflated prices because the corner store is the only SNAP-authorized retailer within walking distance. A parent with $23 left on the card for the week grabs the calorie-dense, nutrient-poor option that fills three kids’ stomachs through dinner, and the grocery budget stretches another two days.

Soda and snack companies engineer their products to hook you. The formulations hit specific ratios of sugar, fat, and salt that trigger dopamine responses, and a family spending its limited benefits on those products ends up buying the very thing that makes the next doctor’s visit more expensive. The program’s structure also punishes a parent working two jobs who gets home at 9 p.m. and opens a package of instant noodles or slides a frozen pizza into the oven because there’s no time or energy left to chop an onion. A child in that household develops fatty liver disease at six times the rate of a kid in an affluent suburb, and the cognitive damage from that condition can shave 18% off lifetime earnings. Dr. Nadine Burke Harris put it plainly in her testimony: “We’re condemning a generation to shortened, sicker lives because Coca-Cola won’t give up its revenue stream.” The obesity-related medical expenses tied to this arrangement run $102 billion a year, and the corporation that profits from the soda purchase pays none of it. The bill lands on the family, the county clinic, and the taxpayer who funded the original benefit in the first place.

### **The Politics of Hunger: Recent Reforms and the Attack on SNAP**

SNAP used to be a conversation about access, about enrollment numbers and the stigma of swiping an EBT card at a grocery checkout. That conversation is over. The current fight in Washington runs on two tracks, and they don’t share a goal. One track asks the USDA to let states block soda and candy purchases with benefits. The other pushes to shrink the program’s budget or eliminate it. The first track keeps the public argument focused on candy bars while the second one rewrites the funding. A small cluster of legislators wants SNAP to function as a nutrition intervention. A larger coalition wants it gone. Both fights are playing out in the same congressional session, and the second one has more money and more votes behind it.

The nutrition track has policy momentum behind it. In 2025, Arkansas, Indiana, Iowa, and Nebraska filed waiver requests with the USDA, asking for permission to bar SNAP recipients from buying soda and candy with their benefits. The Trump administration backed the idea. Robert F. Kennedy Jr., running Health and Human Services, and Brooke Rollins at Agriculture both signaled support for the waivers. Then the industry pushed back. After the USDA approved Indiana’s waiver, the American Beverage Association spent $790,000 on lobbying in the first quarter of 2025. That was their highest quarterly spend since 2010. Senator Mike Lee (R-UT) introduced the Healthy SNAP Act of 2025 to write these restrictions into federal law, banning SNAP purchases of soft drinks and candy, and extending that ban to prepared desserts. Proponents say the program should steer people toward food a doctor would recommend, and that doing so trims long-term healthcare spending.

But the nutrition fight is a sideshow next to what the OBB does. Congress passed the “One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025,” a budget reconciliation package, and the president signed it on July 4, 2025. The bill projects $300 billion in SNAP cuts over ten years. That number hollows out the nutrition title of the farm bill. The waiver requests and the Lee bill, the whole soda-ban conversation, are happening while Congress strips a tenth of the program’s decade-long funding. You can argue about whether a recipient should be able to buy a Coke with benefits, and people are arguing about that, while the budget that pays for those benefits gets rewritten underneath the whole debate. The bill contains several provisions that would severely harm SNAP recipients:

- Work Requirements: The bill expands the Able-Bodied Adults Without Dependents (ABAWD) work requirements to adults aged 18-64, including those with children. It eliminates crucial exemptions for veterans, homeless individuals, and youth aging out of foster care. This could lead to approximately 4 million people losing some or all of their benefits when fully implemented.

- Benefit Cuts: The law freezes the Thrifty Food Plan (TFP), the basis for calculating SNAP benefits, at its 2021 inflation-adjusted level, preventing future updates that would otherwise increase benefits. This is the largest SNAP cut in nearly 30 years and will slash average monthly benefits.

- Cost-Shifting: The bill shifts the financial burden of the program onto states. Starting in fiscal year 2027, states will receive only 25% federal reimbursement for administrative costs (down from 50%), and beginning in FY 2028, states with high payment error rates will be required to contribute 5-15% of benefit costs.

- Defunding Key Programs: The OBB ends mandatory funding for the SNAP-Ed nutrition education program starting in fiscal year 2026, a program proven to improve dietary choices.

- Immigration Restrictions: The law cuts off SNAP eligibility for most refugees, asylees, and survivors of trafficking and domestic violence, affecting tens of thousands of people.

Conservative think tanks, the Heritage Foundation chief among them, push these cuts. Their “Mandate for Leadership 2025” wants to convert SNAP into a discretionary block grant and eliminate broad-based categorical eligibility (BBCE), stripping states of the flexibility to expand access. The same document calls for rolling back TFP updates. The Heritage Foundation treats SNAP as a welfare program you reform or defund, a budget line to trim. Democrats can’t agree on how to respond, and the split weakens their position. Some oppose soda restrictions because they worry the restrictions hand conservatives a talking point about poor people making bad choices. Others look at the proposed cuts and call them a betrayal of the country’s obligation to feed people who can’t feed themselves. Progressives push for healthier food requirements while conservatives want to shrink the whole apparatus, and the version where SNAP keeps doing what it’s done for decades has no real champion in either camp.

### **Pathways to Redemption: Evidence-Based Solutions for a Just Food System**

SNAP can be fixed, and the fixes already exist in pilot data from Massachusetts and comparative studies out of Mexico. You’d need to do two things at once: pull corporate money out of the rulemaking process, and raise benefits high enough that a family can buy food that won’t make them sick. Doing one without the other moves the problem around without solving it. The structural changes require politicians willing to tell Coca-Cola and PepsiCo their lobbying dollars don’t buy seats at the table anymore. The program’s full statutory name is Supplemental NUTRITION Assistance. Right now the program handles the “supplemental” and the “assistance” and sort of gestures at the middle word.

The first concrete step is removing soda, energy drinks, candy, ice cream, and prepared desserts from SNAP eligibility. The Food Research and Action Center (FRAC) takes funding from Coke, Pepsi, and the American Beverage Association, and they oppose restrictions because they frame SNAP as an income-support program. Coca-Cola and PepsiCo pull $19.86 billion in combined revenue from soda, and they have no interest in watching that number shrink. But 82% of SNAP participants told a 2014 survey they’d support pulling sugary drink benefits. A randomized trial paired those restrictions with fruit and vegetable incentives and measured better diet quality across the board. A microsimulation study ran the numbers on a “SNAP-plus” model, subsidizing healthy foods while penalizing unhealthy ones, and found it produced the largest health gains and cost savings of any option tested. Banning soda alone would prevent 1.2 million diabetes cases over ten years and save an estimated $147 billion in healthcare costs. That figure covers the price of raising benefits to offset the ban, with room left over.

A ban without a benefit increase punishes the people it’s supposed to help. The current benefit levels track the thrifty food plan, which is so low that families end up buying cheap, calorie-dense food because that’s what the money stretches to. Raising benefits to the USDA’s moderate food plan, $450 per person per month, gives a family enough to buy vegetables and lean protein without choosing between that and the electric bill. Massachusetts tested this pairing in a 2021 pilot. Vegetable consumption went up 37%, and hunger rates stayed flat. A 1.5% excise tax on soda sales would cover the cost of the increase, so the manufacturers funding the problem also fund the fix.

Access matters as much as eligibility. The Healthy Food Financing Initiative (HFFI) already exists as a mechanism, but enforcement is thin. Congress could require every SNAP-authorized retailer to stock fresh produce, lean proteins, and whole grains within a set radius of low-income neighborhoods, which would close the gaps where gas stations and dollar stores are the only options. A gas station that can’t meet that standard loses its SNAP authorization. Congress should also lift the ban on using benefits for hot, prepared foods. A working parent getting off a shift at 9 p.m. faces a choice between a $7 processed microwave meal and skipping dinner. Letting that parent buy a rotisserie chicken with SNAP benefits changes the evening in a way that’s hard to overstate. On the political side, Congress should ban food industry contributions to Agriculture Committee members and build a transparent review structure into the rulemaking process. A SNAP Nutrition Council with voting seats for public health experts and beneficiaries would put the people affected by the rules in the room where the rules get written. Mexico’s soda tax and the WIC nutrition overhaul both produced measurable health outcomes after implementation. The United States has the same policy tools available and the same data to justify using them.

### **Conclusion**

SNAP is corrupt through its whole structure, and the fixes are already sitting in the data. Congress needs to pull corporate money out of the rulemaking process and point the program toward food that keeps beneficiaries healthy. Pilot programs in Massachusetts and comparative studies out of Mexico proved both moves work. The evidence has been available for years. The holdup is the food and beverage industry’s grip on the legislators who write SNAP rules. The program’s full statutory name is Supplemental NUTRITION Assistance Program, and the program has treated that word “Nutrition” as optional for years.

Pulling soda, energy drinks, candy, ice cream, and prepared desserts off the SNAP eligibility list addresses the single largest driver of diet-related disease among beneficiaries. Anti-hunger groups like the Food Research and Action Center (FRAC) take funding from Coke, Pepsi, and the American Beverage Association, then oppose exactly this kind of restriction on the grounds that SNAP functions as an income-support program. You can trace that opposition to the $19.86 billion in combined soda revenue that Coca-Cola and PepsiCo generate. The beneficiaries themselves disagree. A 2014 survey found 82% of SNAP participants wanted sugary drinks removed from the program. A randomized trial confirmed that pairing those restrictions with fruit and vegetable incentives improved diet quality. A microsimulation study went a step further and found that a “SNAP-plus” model, one that subsidizes healthy foods while penalizing unhealthy ones, produced the largest health gains and cost savings of any option tested. A soda ban specifically would prevent 1.2 million diabetes cases over ten years and save an estimated $147 billion in healthcare costs. That sum covers the price of raising benefits to offset the ban several times over.

A ban with no offset punishes the people using the program. Current benefit levels track the USDA’s thrifty food plan, and that plan stretches so thin that families end up choosing between rent and a bag of apples. Raising benefits to the USDA’s moderate food plan, $450 per person per month, gives households the purchasing power to buy lean protein and fresh produce without rationing. Massachusetts ran this exact combination in a 2021 pilot. Vegetable consumption went up 37%. Hunger did not increase. A 1.5% excise tax on soda sales would fund the benefit increase and put the cost on the manufacturers who built their margins selling sugar water to low-income households.

Product eligibility is half the problem, and access is the other half. The Healthy Food Financing Initiative (HFFI) already exists on paper, but no agency enforces it. Congress should require every SNAP-authorized retailer within a set radius of low-income neighborhoods to stock fresh produce, lean proteins, and whole grains. Gas stations that can’t meet that standard lose their SNAP authorization. The ban on using benefits for hot, prepared foods also needs to go. A working parent getting off a shift at 9 p.m. faces a choice between a $7 processed microwave meal and skipping dinner, and letting that parent buy a rotisserie chicken with SNAP benefits is a small administrative change with a large practical effect. The political structure around SNAP needs the same kind of overhaul. Congress should ban food industry contributions to Agriculture Committee members and establish a transparent, public process for reviewing and changing SNAP rules. A SNAP Nutrition Council with voting seats for public health experts and beneficiaries would put the people affected by the rules in the room where the rules get written. Mexico’s soda tax and the WIC nutrition overhaul both produced measurable public health results, and the United States has the pilot data and the international precedent sitting on the shelf. The question is whether a sitting legislator will spend the political capital to defund the pipeline of poison.
